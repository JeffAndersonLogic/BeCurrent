Topic 3 · Reverse History · Monday, Sept. 14

Why the U.S. and Iran Became Enemies

FRONTLINE showed you the consequences. Today we hit rewind. Start with the 1979 revolution and hostage crisis, then go farther back to the 1953 coup. Your job is not to memorize two dates—it is to decide how each changed the relationship.

Today’s question

Which transformed U.S.-Iran relations more: 1953 or 1979?

A strong answer can choose either year, but it must explain the mechanism.

Reverse move
Effects first. Causes second.
Reverse stop 1 · 1979

An American ally becomes an adversary.

The Shah had been a major U.S. partner. A broad revolution overthrew him, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini became the central figure of the new Islamic Republic, and the U.S. Embassy hostage crisis turned political rupture into a lasting American public grievance.

1941–1979

Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi

Iran’s monarch and a close U.S. partner. His government pursued rapid modernization but also political repression, including through the SAVAK security service. Different opposition groups united against him for different reasons.

Image: public domain via Wikimedia Commons
1979

Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini

Exiled cleric and revolutionary leader who became the Islamic Republic’s first Supreme Leader. The new system rejected the Shah’s alliance with Washington and defined itself partly against foreign domination.

Image: public domain via Wikimedia Commons

Three things change fast.

The Shah falls

A pillar of U.S. strategy in the region disappears.

The Islamic Republic forms

Iran’s political system and foreign-policy identity change dramatically.

The hostage crisis begins

On Nov. 4, 1979, militants seize the U.S. Embassy. Fifty-two Americans are held for 444 days.

Reverse stop 2 · 1953

Why did the revolution carry such a powerful memory of foreign intervention?

To answer that, go back twenty-six years. Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh nationalized Iran’s oil industry. A political crisis followed, and U.S. and British intelligence supported a covert operation that helped remove him and strengthen the Shah.

1951–1953

Mohammad Mosaddegh

Iranian prime minister who nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. For supporters, nationalization represented sovereignty over a strategic resource; opponents feared instability, communist influence, and loss of Western interests.

Image: International News Photos, public domain in U.S. via Wikimedia Commons
AFTER 1953

The Shah returns stronger

The monarchy survived and became more closely aligned with Washington. That relationship later made U.S. support for the Shah part of the revolutionary movement’s critique of foreign interference.

Image: public domain in Iran via Wikimedia Commons
Filing 2 · Historical memory

Why did 1953 remain politically powerful during and after the revolution?

Do not answer “because Iran remembered it.” Explain what the memory allowed later actors to believe or argue about the United States.

Evidence station

What can a declassified U.S. record actually prove?

The U.S. Office of the Historian now documents American and British covert involvement. That establishes intervention. It does not mean foreign intelligence controlled every Iranian actor or caused every later event.

Use the archive with limits.

The official Foreign Relations of the United States volume on Iran, 1951–1954 includes records showing U.S. planning and involvement in the operation against Mosaddegh. That is strong evidence for the claim that the United States intervened in the crisis.

Source discipline: “The United States helped organize a covert operation” is supported. “The CIA single-handedly created everything that happened in Iran afterward” is not.

Perspective check

Two countries carry different memories forward.

These memories are not mirror images, but both matter because leaders and publics use past events to interpret later threats.

Iranian political memory

1953

Foreign powers helped remove a prime minister after oil nationalization and strengthened the Shah. Later critics of the monarchy used that history as evidence that Washington would interfere in Iranian sovereignty.

⇄
American political memory

1979–1981

The embassy seizure and 444-day hostage crisis made revolutionary Iran synonymous for many Americans with humiliation, danger, and hostility toward the United States.

Filing 4 · Comparison

What did the hostage crisis add to American historical memory that 1953 had added to Iranian memory?

Claim builder

Which transformed U.S.-Iran relations more: 1953 or 1979?

Choose one. Then concede what the other year explains better. The goal is causal weight, not a one-sided indictment.
