Topic 3 · Reverse History · Monday, Sept. 14

Why the U.S. and Iran Became Enemies

FRONTLINE showed you the consequences. Today we trace the hostility to two turning points. First, go back to 1953 and the fight over oil, sovereignty, and Operation Ajax. Then move forward to the 1979 Islamic Revolution and the hostage crisis. Your job is to decide what each event added to the distrust that followed.

1953→1979→LONG MEMORY→2026
Today’s question

Which transformed U.S.-Iran relations more: 1953 or 1979?

A strong answer can choose either year, but it must explain the mechanism and use evidence from both.

Today’s route
Quick origins. Then 1953. Then 1979.
Quick setup · Before 1953

Iran was not created by the Mandate System.

After World War I, Britain and France governed several former Ottoman territories through League of Nations mandates. Iran followed a different path. Persia was already an independent state with a long political history, although Britain and Russia repeatedly competed for influence there. That difference matters because the 1953 crisis grew out of arguments over sovereignty and foreign interference, not out of a newly created mandate state.

Three things to know before the oil crisis.

1 · The mandate system

It reshaped much of the Arab Middle East after World War I.

Britain received mandates over places including Iraq and Palestine, while France received Syria and Lebanon. Those arrangements placed former Ottoman territories under European administration.

2 · Iran was different

Persia was not a League of Nations mandate.

It remained formally independent, but British and Russian power still mattered enormously. Foreign pressure, oil concessions, and repeated intervention made sovereignty a major political issue.

3 · The Pahlavi monarchy

Reza Shah built a stronger centralized state.

Reza Khan took power in the 1920s and became Reza Shah Pahlavi. In 1935, the government asked foreign countries to use the name Iran. In 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union forced him to abdicate, and his son Mohammad Reza Shah became monarch — the Shah students will encounter in 1953 and 1979.

Historical root · 1951 to 1953

Oil, sovereignty, and Operation Ajax.

The U.S.-Iran conflict did not begin in 1979. A generation earlier, a struggle over who controlled Iran’s oil became a struggle over political power. That episode later became one of the most important Iranian memories of American interference.

Four moves to understand before the coup.

1 · Oil

A foreign company dominated Iran’s oil industry.

The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company was British controlled. Many Iranians believed the arrangement denied Iran a fair share of the wealth produced from its own resource.

2 · Nationalization

Mosaddegh challenged that system.

Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh became the leading figure in a movement to nationalize Iran’s oil industry in 1951. Supporters treated nationalization as a question of sovereignty.

3 · Crisis

Britain pushed back.

Britain resisted nationalization and applied intense economic and diplomatic pressure. The crisis deepened while the Cold War made U.S. officials increasingly worried about instability and communist influence.

4 · Covert action

U.S. and British intelligence backed a plan to remove Mosaddegh.

In 1953, Operation TPAJAX helped undermine Mosaddegh and strengthen Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. The monarchy survived and became a much closer U.S. partner.

1951–1953

Mohammad Mosaddegh

Iranian prime minister and leading advocate of oil nationalization. His supporters saw the issue as national control over a strategic resource. His opponents feared economic breakdown, political instability, and communist influence.

The record CAN establish

Foreign intervention was real.

  • U.S. and British officials planned and authorized Operation TPAJAX.
  • The covert operation was intended to change Iran's political situation and remove Mosaddegh.
  • American involvement is therefore a documented part of the 1953 story.
The record CANNOT establish by itself

It does not explain everything.

  • That the CIA controlled every Iranian who participated.
  • That foreign intervention was the coup's only cause.
  • That 1953 automatically made the 1979 Revolution — or the 2026 war — inevitable.

Turning point · 1978 to 1979

The revolution was bigger than one man and bigger than one grievance.

By the late 1970s, the Shah was still a major U.S. ally, but opposition to his rule had spread across very different groups. Students, secular nationalists, leftists, merchants, religious leaders, and many ordinary Iranians opposed him for different reasons. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini became the most powerful revolutionary leader, but the movement that brought down the Shah was broader than his followers alone.

How the revolution unfolded.

Before 1978

Modernization and repression existed together.

The Shah promoted major economic and social reforms and built a strong state. His government also restricted political opposition, and the SAVAK security service became a symbol of surveillance, imprisonment, and abuse.

1978

Protests became a cycle.

Demonstrations spread. When protesters were killed, later mourning gatherings often produced new demonstrations. Opposition grew while the government struggled to restore control.

Jan.–Feb. 1979

The monarchy collapsed.

The Shah left Iran in January. Khomeini returned from exile on February 1. The armed forces declared neutrality, the monarchy fell, and revolutionary groups competed to shape the new order.

After the fall

The Islamic Republic consolidated power.

Khomeini and his allies built a new Islamic Republic and pushed aside many former revolutionary partners. The new political system rejected the Shah’s close alignment with Washington.

1941–1979

Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi

Iran’s monarch and a close U.S. partner. To supporters, he represented modernization and stability. To many opponents, he represented authoritarian rule, inequality, and a government protected by foreign power.

Image: public domain via Wikimedia Commons
1979

Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini

Exiled cleric and revolutionary leader who returned to enormous popular support and became the Islamic Republic’s first Supreme Leader. He framed the Shah’s alliance with the United States as part of a wider problem of foreign domination.

1979–1981 · The hostage crisis

The revolution broke the alliance. The hostage crisis burned the rupture into American memory.

The Shah had already lost power and left Iran in January 1979. After months in exile, President Jimmy Carter allowed him to enter the United States in October for cancer treatment. For many Iranians shaped by the memory of the 1953 coup, his arrival revived fears that Washington might again intervene to restore him or otherwise shape Iran’s politics. On November 4, student militants seized the U.S. Embassy in Tehran.

Why this became a second turning point.

Nov. 4, 1979

The embassy is seized.

Student militants took American diplomats and staff hostage. Khomeini backed the takeover, turning a student action into a central confrontation between the new Iranian order and the United States.

444 days

52 Americans remain captive.

The crisis dominated American news and politics. A failed U.S. rescue attempt in April 1980 deepened the sense of humiliation and helplessness.

Jan. 20, 1981

The hostages are released.

The Americans were freed after 444 days, but the crisis had transformed how millions of Americans understood revolutionary Iran.

Perspective check

Two countries learned different stories about when the hostility began.

Historical memory matters because people do not interpret later threats from a blank slate. Each side carried a different event forward as evidence about what the other side might do.

Iranian political memory

1953 + the Shah

Foreign intelligence helped remove Mosaddegh during an oil and sovereignty crisis, and Washington then maintained a close relationship with the Shah. That history made later U.S. actions easier for revolutionaries to interpret as possible interference.

⇄
American political memory

1979–1981

The embassy seizure and 444-day hostage crisis made revolutionary Iran synonymous for many Americans with humiliation, danger, and hostility toward the United States.

The trap to avoid.

1953 does not make the 1979 revolution or hostage crisis inevitable. The hostage crisis does not erase the history that preceded it. A stronger explanation asks how each event changed expectations, fear, and trust, then shows how those memories influenced later choices.

Claim builder

Which transformed U.S.-Iran relations more: 1953 or 1979?

Choose one. Then concede what the other year explains better. The goal is causal weight, not a one-sided indictment.
