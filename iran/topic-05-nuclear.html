Topic 5 · Reverse History thread · 1950s–2026

The Nuclear Bargain

Iran and the United States once cooperated on nuclear technology. Decades later, they built a deal to limit Iran's nuclear program. Then the deal broke down. Topic 5 follows one question: did that breakdown make war more likely?

Today's question

Did the collapse of the 2015 nuclear agreement make eventual war substantially more likely?

Follow the story first. Then decide what the evidence shows.

Reverse reveal
Who helped Iran start a nuclear program? The United States.
Chapter 1 · The surprise

The United States helped Iran start.

Iran's nuclear program began while Iran and the United States were allies.

1957

Nuclear cooperation begins

The United States and Iran sign an agreement for peaceful uses of atomic energy.

1967

U.S.-supplied reactor opens

A U.S.-supplied research reactor begins operating in Tehran.

Big idea

Iran's nuclear program did not begin as an anti-American weapons project. It began during a period of U.S.-Iran cooperation.

Chapter 2 · The break

1979 changes everything.

The technology stays. The political relationship changes.

1979

The Iranian Revolution

The Shah is overthrown. Iran's new Islamic government and the United States become bitter rivals.

Ally → Enemy

The revolution did not erase Iran's nuclear knowledge. It changed who controlled the program and how the United States viewed it.

Chapter 3 · The fear

Could Iran eventually build a bomb?

By the early 2000s, new information about Iranian nuclear facilities increases international concern.

2002+

Undeclared facilities become public

Facilities at Natanz and Arak draw greater scrutiny, inspections and debate over Iran's intentions.

The question changes

Iran says its program is peaceful. Other governments worry the same technology could create a future weapons option.

Chapter 4 · The bargain

2015: Let's make a deal.

The JCPOA trades nuclear limits and inspections for sanctions relief.

Iran gives

Limits + inspections

Iran accepts restrictions on enrichment, its enriched-uranium stockpile and some nuclear equipment. International inspectors monitor the program.

2015
Other countries give

Sanctions relief

Nuclear-related sanctions are lifted or suspended as Iran meets its commitments.

Big idea

The deal tried to control the nuclear problem. It did not try to solve every U.S.-Iran conflict.

Keep the scope straight: Missiles, armed partners, regional conflict and other disputes remained outside the main nuclear bargain.

Why the deal was limited

One problem at a time

Supporters argued a narrower nuclear agreement was more achievable. Critics argued that leaving other Iranian behavior outside the deal made it too weak.

Chapter 5 · The breakdown

2018: The United States leaves.

President Donald Trump ends U.S. participation and restores sanctions.

Withdrawal argument

The deal was too narrow and temporary.

The Trump administration argued that the JCPOA did not permanently block Iran's nuclear path and did not address missiles or Iran's regional armed partners.

2018
Keep-the-deal argument

Verified limits were better than no limits.

Supporters argued that inspections and measurable restrictions reduced nuclear risk even if other disputes remained.

Causation check

Not one cause

Withdrawal changed incentives, but later Iranian, U.S., Israeli and other decisions also shaped the path toward conflict.

The key tension

Pressure or trust?

Sanctions and military threats can create leverage. They can also reduce trust in negotiated promises and increase the value leaders place on deterrence.

Chapter 6 · Back to today

Diplomacy gives way to force.

By the mid-2020s, the nuclear dispute is no longer only about negotiations and inspections.

2025

Military attacks reach nuclear sites

Israel launches a war against Iran, and the United States strikes Iranian nuclear sites.

2026

The nuclear question remains unresolved

The nuclear program remains part of diplomacy even after major fighting.

We are back where we started.

The argument is no longer just about uranium. It is about whether the breakdown of a negotiated system helped move the conflict closer to war.

Historical tension

More pressure can cut both ways.

Pressure can raise the cost of nuclear activity. It can also make negotiated promises less credible to Iranian leaders.

Remember

The nuclear issue is one part of a larger conflict.

Missiles, regional armed groups, Iran-Israel hostility and other disputes remained important throughout this period.

Think 3 · Final claim

Did the collapse of the nuclear agreement make eventual war substantially more likely?

Use at least two moments from the story. You may argue yes, no, or “yes, but only when combined with another factor.”

A student could argue

Yes.

The agreement created limits and inspections. Its collapse was followed by expanding nuclear activity, greater fear and more military pressure.

OR
A student could argue

Not by itself.

Missiles, armed groups, Iran-Israel hostility and regional conflict could still have produced war even if the nuclear deal survived.
