Topic 7 · Geography becomes power

The Hormuz Lever

A narrow waterway between Iran and Oman carries enough energy traffic that conflict there can change prices, shipping routes and diplomacy far beyond the Persian Gulf. Geography gives Iran a form of leverage that does not require matching the U.S. Navy ship for ship.

Today's question

Is control of Hormuz mainly a military weapon, an economic weapon or a diplomatic bargaining tool?

The answer can be more than one—but one function has to do the most explanatory work.

Reverse move
How can forty miles of water affect countries thousands of miles away?
Step 1 · Chokepoint

There is only one sea exit from the Persian Gulf.

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the wider Indian Ocean. Iran lies along the north side; Oman controls the Musandam Peninsula on the south.

Read the map as strategy

Three things matter.

Narrow routeShips entering or leaving the Gulf pass through a small geographic space that is easier to threaten than an open ocean.
Major producers behind itSaudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, the UAE and Iran all depend to varying degrees on Gulf export routes.
Alternatives exist—but not enoughSome pipelines can bypass Hormuz, but alternative capacity cannot simply replace every barrel or LNG shipment that normally moves through the strait.

Step 2 · The Tanker War

The Iran-Iraq War expands into commercial shipping.

Beginning in the 1980s, both Iran and Iraq attacked shipping connected to the other's oil trade. The conflict drew outside navies more directly into the Persian Gulf.

1984

Attacks on tankers intensify

Iraq attacks ships serving Iranian oil facilities; Iran retaliates against Gulf shipping associated with Iraq's supporters.

1987

U.S. escorts enter the picture

Kuwaiti tankers are reflagged under the U.S. flag and escorted in Operation Earnest Will as mines and attacks threaten commercial traffic.

APR 1988

Mine strike and U.S.-Iran naval combat

After the USS Samuel B. Roberts hits an Iranian mine, the United States launches Operation Praying Mantis against Iranian naval targets.

LESSON

Shipping can internationalize a regional war

A conflict between Iran and Iraq pulls major outside powers toward direct military involvement because global commerce is passing through the same water.

Step 3 · 2026 data

The scale of the disruption is measurable.

Instead of relying on a dramatic headline, use tanker-tracking estimates from the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

21.6M

barrels per day

Estimated crude oil and petroleum liquids transported through Hormuz in the fourth quarter of 2025, before the 2026 conflict.

4.9M

barrels per day

Estimated average in the second quarter of 2026 after conflict sharply disrupted traffic through the strait.

That is the leverage.

The EIA numbers do not tell you Iran's motive. They show why threats, mines, attacks and shipping restrictions around Hormuz can matter economically. A disruption forces buyers to hunt for alternative supply, changes tanker routes, affects production inside Gulf states and can push energy prices higher.

By August 26, 2026, Iran and Oman were discussing a temporary arrangement for commercial shipping through Iranian and Omani waters. Iranian officials said military vessels would not be allowed under the proposed arrangement. That makes Hormuz part of diplomacy as well as military strategy.

Current-event rule: The exact shipping arrangement is living content. The chokepoint geography and Tanker War history are stable; the August 2026 negotiation is the layer the Iran monitoring task should re-check.

Perspective check

The same chokepoint creates different interests.

No actor experiences Hormuz as “just geography.”

Iran

Leverage against stronger powers

A state with fewer conventional military resources can make threats to commerce part of deterrence and negotiation.

United States

Freedom of navigation

Washington treats unimpeded commercial shipping and the ability of naval forces to operate in international waters as major security interests.

Gulf exporters and importers

Keep the route open

States may disagree about Iran or U.S. policy while sharing an immediate interest in moving energy and commercial traffic safely.
