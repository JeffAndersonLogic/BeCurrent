The Battle for Congress

55 minutes before lunch · 20–25 minutes of news after lunch · Civics

Teacher briefing / goals, story, and preparation

How can a few seats change the decisions Congress considers?

Learning targets

  • I can explain how individual elections determine chamber membership.
  • I can explain how a majority affects leaders and committees.
  • I can distinguish organizing a chamber from passing a law.

Success criteria

  • I can identify the majority in a constructed 435-seat House scenario.
  • I can identify two decisions a new majority could influence.
  • I can explain one reason chamber control does not guarantee a policy change.

The story to tell

Move from the ballot to the working room. Separate races produce a chamber. A small difference in seats can decide who organizes that chamber. Leaders and committees can change which issues receive attention before a final vote. Keep the seat model explicitly hypothetical. Then add the limit: one chamber still needs the other chamber and ordinarily presidential action to enact a law.

Watch for this misconception

The 217/218 model is not a current seat count. House control does not guarantee Senate agreement.

Prepare before students arrive

  • Use the background as a fallback if a clip is blocked.

Assessment notes and access options

Can influence; Can influence; Not guaranteed; Can influence; Not guaranteed. Connect a seat change to a leadership decision and a limit.

Look for accurate facts, attributed evidence, and reasoning. Accept concise writing, voice typing, or the printable paper activity under the student’s accommodations. No student report-out is required. The teacher can voice anonymous examples.

Suggested checkpoint rubric, 20 points: accurate institutional facts (6); source and evidence (6); cause-and-effect reasoning with a limit (6); clear response (2). Practice errors can be corrected.
