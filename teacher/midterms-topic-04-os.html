Teacher briefing / goals, story, and preparation

What should we check before believing an election message?

Learning targets

  • I can explain how a campaign message directs attention.
  • I can check a claim against an independent source.
  • I can distinguish a poll, a promise, and a verified result.

Success criteria

  • I can identify a message’s claim, audience, and missing context.
  • I can name a second source and explain what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
  • I can explain why a poll or promise does not establish what will happen.

The story to tell

Begin with a short teacher introduction to today's topic. Then play the videos back to back: Modern Political Campaigns, the PBS NewsHour attack-ads report, the CBS Detroit political-ads report, and the WFYI research-candidates explainer; add the three FactCheck.org ad examples if time allows. Then release students to the eight-question worksheet: examine two campaign ads, identify framing, check CMS/CBO/FactCheck.org, and file their own findings in Canvas. Polls and promises are not results.

Watch for this misconception

Do not grade political preferences or treat repeated press-release coverage as independent corroboration.

Assessment notes and access options

Campaign promise; Poll estimate; Official result; Claim needing checking; Claim needing checking. Assess source specificity and the match between evidence and conclusion.

Look for accurate facts, attributed evidence, and reasoning. Accept concise writing, voice typing, or the printable paper activity under the student’s accommodations. No student report-out is required. The teacher can voice anonymous examples.

How this investigation is assessed / 5 points

Political preferences are not scored. Short writing, dictated text, and agreed paper responses can demonstrate the same learning.

  • Campaign purpose and video. Explain why campaigns persuade and identify a messaging technique.
  • Ad analysis. Identify both ads' messages, a persuasive choice, and a checkable claim.
  • Evidence and revision. Name a relevant independent source, identify a limit or omitted context, and update the first impression.
  • Poll and promise limits. Distinguish sampled opinion and proposed policy from verified outcomes.
