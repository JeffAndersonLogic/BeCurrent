The Election Nobody Understands

55 minutes before lunch · 20–25 minutes of news after lunch · Civics

Student view ↗Open projector ↗
Teacher briefing / goals, story, and preparation

If the president is not on the ballot, why can the midterms change who has power?

Learning targets

  • I can explain what a midterm election changes and what it does not.
  • I can distinguish House elections from Senate elections.
  • I can explain one way a changed congressional majority could affect government decisions.

Success criteria

  • I can identify congressional offices on the ballot and explain that the presidency is not being elected.
  • I can sort at least four of five House/Senate facts correctly and correct my errors.
  • I can use one verified fact to explain a possible governing consequence.

The story to tell

Begin with the missing president on the ballot. Then reveal the people who consider laws, decide spending through legislation, and investigate government. The House is elected all at once every two years; the Senate turns over in parts. Those elections can leave a president facing new partners or opponents. The outcome changes the setting for decisions. It does not guarantee a policy.

Watch for this misconception

Not all 100 Senate seats are on the ballot. A new House majority does not remove the president.

Prepare before students arrive

  • Open the student lesson, projector window, and assigned clip windows. Check captions and the reading fallback.
  • Use the background as a fallback if a clip is blocked.
  • Have the Canvas Text Entry assignment ready; the event and assignment copy tools are at the bottom of this page.
  • Finish unit work before lunch. Keep lunch untimed. Return to CNN10, then The Desk.

Assessment notes and access options

House; Senate; Senate; Both; Both. A strong response connects membership to leadership, agenda, or oversight and gives a limit.

Look for accurate facts, attributed evidence, and reasoning. Accept concise writing, voice typing, or the printable paper activity under the student’s accommodations. No student report-out is required. The teacher can voice anonymous examples.

How this investigation is assessed / 5 points

Political preferences are not scored. Short writing, dictated text, and agreed paper responses can demonstrate the same learning.

  • What voters choose. Correctly identify Congress as the House and Senate, distinguish their election cycles, and explain that the presidency is not being elected.
  • Fact and source. Name a relevant source, accurately use a fact it establishes, and identify something it cannot establish yet.
  • Power and limit. Connect a changed majority to leadership, priorities, or oversight and explain one change it does not automatically cause.
  • Explain your thinking. Make the connection understandable in concise writing, dictated text, or the agreed paper response. Spelling and length do not determine this score.
