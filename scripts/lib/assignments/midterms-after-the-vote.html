Midterms · After the Vote

Why They Lost, and What Comes Next

OVERVIEW

Two articles from The Week, published after the midterms. The first collects writers' explanations for why Republicans lost the House and Senate. The second covers how President Trump and both parties are responding. Read both, then answer four questions on each. You will weigh explanations, sort reporting from opinion, and take positions using evidence.

ARTICLE 1

Read: Midterms: Explaining the GOP’s crushing defeat (The Week)

1. Which explanation holds up best? (Causation)
The article gives several reasons Republicans lost: the Iran war and gas prices, corruption, how Democrats campaigned, Latino voters shifting, and “all the things” in Trump’s second term. Which explanation does the article back up with the strongest evidence? What evidence would you still want before you were sure?
Start here: “The strongest explanation is ___ because the article shows ___. I would still want to know ___.”

2. Who is doing the explaining? (Sourcing)
Every explanation comes from an opinion writer at a different outlet, such as National Review, The Atlantic, The Free Press and RealClearPolitics. Pick one writer. Why might that writer’s outlet or point of view affect which cause they chose to blame?
Start here: “___ writes for ___, which might make them want readers to believe ___.”

3. What do the numbers actually prove? (Generalizing from Evidence)
Exit polls found about 90% of voters named the cost of living and 60% called the Iran war a mistake. What do those numbers tell us for certain, and what can’t they tell us? Can a poll explain why a person voted the way they did?
Start here: “The poll proves ___, but it doesn’t prove ___.”

4. The lesson for next time (Taking a Position)
Imagine you are advising either party before the 2028 election. What is the one lesson from this midterm you would tell them, and which piece of evidence from the article supports it?
Start here: “If I advised the ___ party, I would tell them ___ because ___.”

ARTICLE 2

Read: Trump and Democrats brace for battle (The Week)

5. Change course or double down? (Causation)
After a big loss, a leader can change direction or push harder in the same direction. After the midterms, Trump named Stephen Miller his chief of staff. Why do the writers in the article think Trump made that choice? Do you think it is a smart move or a risky one, and why?
Start here: “The article suggests Trump picked Miller because ___. I think this is ___ because ___.”

6. Checking power or harassing? (Civics)
Democrats take control of the House and Senate in January and plan investigations, including letters asking companies like Amazon, Apple and Walmart for records. The White House’s lawyer warned that investigations meant to harass could cross a legal line. Congress is supposed to keep a check on the president. Where is the line between doing that job and harassing?
Start here: “An investigation is fair when ___. It turns into harassment when ___.”

7. Fact or opinion? (Sourcing)
This article mixes two kinds of writing. Some writers report what happened, such as Justice Department officials leaving their jobs. Others argue or predict, such as Fred Kaplan saying Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is “doomed.” Find one more example of each. How can you tell the difference?
Start here: “A fact in the article is ___. An opinion is ___. I can tell because ___.”

8. Stay loyal or step away? (Taking a Position)
House Republicans chose Jim Jordan, a strong Trump supporter, to lead them, even after losing badly. Should Republicans in Congress stay close to Trump or put some distance between themselves and him? Use evidence from either article.
Start here: “I think Republicans should ___ because ___.”
