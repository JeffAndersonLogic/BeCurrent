a person still make, and who gets to set the rules?

with headphones, notes boxes printed on the page (no copying required), eight numbered
questions, Gather All My Work, Canvas before lunch. CNN 10 and the News Log after lunch.

read this chat."* Every MagicSchool step also has sample answers printed on the page, so the
lesson works if the room is down or a student is absent.

## Topic 1: What AI Actually Is
**Subtitle:** A very good guesser
**Skill focus:** Generalizing from Evidence
**What students leave knowing:** AI is a computer system that learns patterns from huge amounts
of examples and uses them to make predictions. A chatbot predicts likely next words. Sounding
right and being right are different things.

### The story, in five beats
1. **You already use it.** The For You page from the Social Media unit, autocorrect, face
   unlock, the spam folder. Students have been using AI for years without calling it that.
2. **Rules versus learning.** A calculator follows rules a person wrote. A spam filter was shown
   millions of emails and learned what spam tends to look like. *Did a person write every rule,
   or did the machine learn from examples?* That question is the test for "Is this AI?"
3. **Training data decides what it knows.** What it learned from shapes what it can do, and
   what it gets wrong. Its knowledge also stops at a date.
4. **How a chatbot talks.** It was trained on enormous amounts of writing and produces one likely
   word after another. That is why it sounds smooth and confident. Some chatbots can also
   search the web, but the answer is still written by prediction.
5. **Why this is news now.** Just over half of U.S. teens say they have used a chatbot for help
   with schoolwork, and 59% think using AI to cheat is common at their school (Pew Research
   Center, February 2026). The last line of the day points to Topic 2: *If it is just
   predicting, can it be wrong and still sound sure?*

### The day
- **Do Now:** You have probably used AI this week. What do you think it actually is? (One
  sentence, private.)
- **Be the Model (paper first):** Finish five sentences: "Peanut butter and ___," "The capital of
  France is ___," "Once upon a ___," "My favorite food is ___," "The best movie ever is ___."
  Students notice which ones everyone answers the same way (predictable) and which they don't.
- **MagicSchool:** Give the chatbot the same five sentence starters. Did it pick the
  predictable word? What did it do with the ones that have no single right answer?
- **AI or Not AI? sort (8 cards):** calculator, TikTok For You page, phone autocorrect, face
  unlock, microwave timer, email spam filter, thermostat set to 70, Google Translate. The rule
  from beat 2 decides each one.
- **Key terms:** artificial intelligence, training data, pattern, prediction, chatbot (large
  language model)

### Draft questions (8)
1. In your own words, what makes something AI instead of an ordinary computer program?
2. Pick one card you sorted as AI. What examples do you think it learned from?
3. Pick one card you sorted as Not AI. How do you know a person wrote its rules?
4. In Be the Model, which sentence did almost everyone finish the same way? Why?
5. What did the chatbot do with a sentence that has no single right answer?
6. Why can a chatbot sound confident about something it got wrong?
7. Pew found just over half of teens have used a chatbot for schoolwork. What is one good use
   and one risky use?
8. **Checkpoint:** "A chatbot is a very good guesser." Do you agree? Use one example from today.

### Sources
- Verified: Pew Research Center, *How Teens Use and View AI* (February 24, 2026).
- To find and check in the sources step: one or two short explainer videos on how machine
  learning and chatbots work (Code.org's *How AI Works* series is the first place to look).

## Topic 2: When AI Gets It Wrong
**Subtitle:** Sounding sure is not the same as being right
**Skill focus:** Sourcing and Corroboration
**What students leave knowing:** AI can make things up (hallucination), repeat the unfairness in
its training data (bias), and fake real people's voices and faces (deepfakes). The habit that
protects you is checking it against a source you can name.

### The story, in five beats
1. **Callback to Topic 1.** A chatbot predicts likely words. Likely is not the same as true, so
   it can produce a confident, detailed answer that is false. That is called a hallucination.
2. **The lawyers who didn't check (2023).** Two New York lawyers filed a court document citing
   court cases that ChatGPT had invented. The cases did not exist. A federal judge fined them
   and their firm $5,000. The lesson is not "AI is bad." It is "they didn't check."
3. **Bias.** If the examples a system learns from are lopsided, its answers are too. (Example to
   confirm in the sources step: Amazon's experimental hiring tool, reported by Reuters in 2018,
   which learned to downgrade résumés that mentioned women's activities.)
4. **The fake president (January 2024).** Before the New Hampshire primary, voters got a robocall
   in an AI-faked version of President Biden's voice telling them not to vote. The FCC fined the
   political consultant behind it $6 million (September 2024). A New Hampshire jury later found
   him not guilty of the state criminal charges (June 2025). This links straight back to the
   Midterms unit: the law is still catching up to the technology.
5. **The habit.** Corroborate. Before you trust it, find a second source you can name.

### The day
- **Do Now:** Have you ever seen something online that turned out to be fake? How did you find
  out? (Private.)
- **MagicSchool, "Catch It Being Wrong":** Students ask the chatbot five questions that have
  checkable answers, then check each one against a real source and label it **Right / Wrong /
  Made up / Can't tell**. Draft question set: something local (who the principal of ZCHS is),
  something recent (what happened in the news yesterday), a counting question ("How many times
  does the letter r appear in *strawberry*?"), a math word problem, and "Give me a real quote
  from a historical figure, with the source."
- **If the chatbot gets everything right**, that is a real finding too. The page then has
  students study printed, well-documented AI mistakes instead.
- **Key terms:** hallucination, bias, deepfake, corroborate

### Draft questions (8)
1. What is a hallucination, in your own words?
2. Why does Topic 1 (prediction) explain why hallucinations happen?
3. In the lawyers' case, what was the real mistake: using AI, or something else?
4. From Catch It Being Wrong: which answer did you check, what source did you use, and what did
   you find?
5. What is bias in an AI system? Where does it come from?
6. Why was the fake Biden robocall dangerous to an election, even though it was caught?
7. The consultant was fined $6 million but found not guilty in court. What does that tell you
   about how ready the law is for deepfakes?
8. **Checkpoint:** Write a three-step rule for checking anything an AI tells you.

### Sources
- Verified: FCC, *FCC Issues $6M Fine For N.H. Robocalls* (September 2024); New Hampshire Public
  Radio on the June 13, 2025 verdict.
- To confirm against the court record in the sources step: *Mata v. Avianca* sanctions order
  (June 2023, U.S. District Court, Southern District of New York).
- To confirm in the sources step: the Reuters 2018 Amazon hiring-tool report.

## Topic 3: What AI Changes, and Who Pays
**Subtitle:** Jobs, power lines, and a field in Lebanon
**Skill focus:** Causation and Framing
**What students leave knowing:** AI usually changes tasks before it replaces whole jobs, and
the early evidence lands hardest on young workers entering the job market. AI also has a
physical cost, and one of the biggest examples in the country is being built in our own county.

### The story, in six beats
1. **Jobs are bundles of tasks.** AI changes some tasks inside a job long before it replaces the
   whole job.
2. **What the research says so far.** Stanford's Digital Economy Lab (August 2026 update) finds
   no widespread job loss across the economy. But workers aged 22 to 25 in jobs most exposed to
   AI are employed at 19% below where they would otherwise be, mostly because companies are
   hiring fewer of them, not firing them. Yale's Budget Lab, tracking the whole job market,
   still sees no clear economy-wide disruption. Both are true. They answer different
   questions, and a headline can pick either one. That is framing.
3. **AI is not "in the cloud."** It runs in data centers: warehouses of computers that need
   huge amounts of electricity and water.
4. **Our county.** On February 11, 2026, Meta broke ground on a roughly $10 billion AI data
   center campus in the LEAP district in Lebanon, in Boone County. Meta says it will bring more
   than 4,000 construction jobs at its peak and about 300 permanent jobs, and that it will pay
   the full cost of the energy it uses. The city of Lebanon approved a property-tax break, and
   the state gave a long sales-tax exemption. A new water program is planned to deliver up to
   25 million gallons a day to Lebanon by 2031.
5. **The pushback.** On June 15, 2026, Boone County commissioners passed a one-year pause on new
   data centers in the county's unincorporated areas, through June 15, 2027, to study the
   effects (it does not affect Meta). Over half of Indiana's counties now restrict data centers
   in some way, and data centers became an issue in the 2026 midterm campaigns.
6. **The tradeoff.** Jobs and tax money on one side; power, water, and land on the other. Who
   gets the benefits, and who pays the costs?

### The day
- **Do Now:** Name a job you might want someday. What is one part of it a computer could
  never do? (Private.)
- **Task, Not Job sort:** Pick one job (for example: restaurant manager, nurse, graphic
  designer, customer service). Break it into five tasks, then label each one *AI could do it /
  AI could help / Needs a person*.
- **MagicSchool:** Ask the chatbot to do one task from your job (for example, write an apology
  email to an upset customer). Would you put your name on what it wrote? What would you change?
- **Cause and effect chain:** AI demand → data center → electricity, water, jobs, tax money →
  who benefits and who pays.
- **Framing compare:** Meta's own announcement versus a local news story about the same Lebanon
  project. What does each one put first? What does each one leave out?
- **Key terms:** automation, data center, tradeoff, moratorium

### Draft questions (8)
1. Why does it make more sense to ask "Which tasks will AI change?" than "Which jobs will AI
   replace?"
2. From your Task, Not Job sort: which task did you mark *Needs a person*, and why?
3. Would you put your name on what the chatbot wrote? Explain.
4. The Stanford and Yale findings sound like they disagree. Explain how both can be true.
5. Why might AI's effect show up first in young workers who are just getting hired?
6. Trace the chain: how does a chatbot answer connect to a field in Lebanon, Indiana?
7. Meta's announcement and the local news story describe the same project. Name one thing each
   one emphasizes.
8. **Checkpoint:** Who benefits most from the Lebanon data center, and who pays the most? Use
   one specific fact. (Your own view is not graded; your evidence is.)

### Sources
- Verified: Meta newsroom and the City of Lebanon (February 11, 2026); Indiana Capital
  Chronicle (February 11, 2026); Boone County government announcement (June 15, 2026); Stanford
  Digital Economy Lab, *Canaries in the Coal Mine?* August 2026 update.
- To confirm from the primary source in the sources step: the Yale Budget Lab's latest tracking
  report; the statewide county-restriction count (Indiana University Environmental Resilience
  Institute, reported by WBAA, October 21, 2026); Indiana Public Radio on data centers as a
  midterm issue (October 2026).

## Topic 4: Who Makes the Rules?
**Subtitle:** What should the rule say?
**Skill focus:** Framing and Generalizing from Evidence
**What students leave knowing:** AI rules come from four places: you, your school, the
companies, and governments. Those rules are still being written, including at our own school,
and students can reason about what a good rule should say.

### The story, in five beats
1. **Four rule-makers.** You (what you choose to type and trust), your school, the companies
   that build the tools, and governments. This echoes the Social Media unit's last topic, *Who
   Gets to Decide?*, with a new technology.
2. **Indiana already has an AI rule, and it connects to Topic 2.** Since 2024, an Indiana
   campaign ad that uses AI to fake a candidate's words or actions must carry a label: *"Elements
   of this media have been digitally altered or artificially generated."* The law requires a
   label instead of a ban, partly because of free-speech concerns. That is a tradeoff students
   can weigh.
3. **States versus Washington.** States have passed a great many AI laws (109 in the first half
   of 2026 alone). In December 2025 the president signed an executive order calling for one
   national approach and set up a Justice Department team to challenge state AI laws in court.
   But an executive order cannot erase a state law by itself; that takes Congress or the courts,
   and Congress has not passed a national AI law. This is the federalism question from Midterms
   Topic 1, applied to a new issue: should AI rules be the same in all 50 states?
4. **Companies make rules too.** Chatbots have minimum ages, topics they refuse, and rules about
   who can see your chats. MagicSchool lets the teacher read student chats; that is a rule
   students have lived with all unit.
5. **Your school is still deciding.** About 40 states, Indiana included, have given schools
   guidance on AI, but guidance is advice, not a rule. Zionsville has not written a student AI
   rule yet. So the last question of the unit is theirs: *What should the rule say?*

### The day
- **Do Now:** Is using AI on homework cheating? *Always, sometimes, or never*, plus one reason.
  (Private.)
- **Who Made This Rule? sort (6 cards):** You / School / Company / Government. Indiana's AI ad
  label; a chatbot's minimum age; MagicSchool letting the teacher read chats; a made-up class
  rule, "No AI on this essay" (labeled made up); Boone County's data center pause (callback to
  Topic 3); choosing never to type private information into a chatbot.
- **MagicSchool:** Ask the chatbot, "Should students be allowed to use AI on homework?" Did it
  take a side? Whose point of view did it leave out? (Framing, using the tool itself as the
  source.)
- **Write the Rule (made-up scenarios, labeled made up):** For each, decide *Allowed / Allowed
  with conditions / Not allowed*, and why:
  1. Using AI to brainstorm ideas for an essay, versus using it to write the essay.
  2. Asking AI to quiz you before a test.
  3. A student makes a fake AI video of the principal announcing a snow day and posts it.
  
  Then write the one-sentence AI rule you would give ZCHS.
- **Last 10 minutes:** Open the study guide for Thursday's exam.
- **Key terms:** regulation, federal and state, disclosure (label), terms of service, policy

### Draft questions (8)
1. Name the four kinds of rule-makers for AI. Which one affects you most on a normal day?
2. Indiana requires a label on AI-faked campaign ads instead of banning them. Give one reason
   lawmakers might choose a label, and one weakness of that choice.
3. Should AI rules be the same in all 50 states? Give one reason for each side, then choose.
4. Why can't a president's executive order, by itself, cancel a state's AI law?
5. From MagicSchool: did the chatbot take a side on homework? What did its answer leave out?
6. Pick one Write the Rule scenario. Explain your decision.
7. The fake snow-day video: who should deal with it, the school, the law, or the app it was
   posted on? Why?
8. **Checkpoint (the unit question):** AI is already making decisions that affect your life.
   Which decisions should a person still make, and who should set the rules? Use one example
   from any topic in the unit. (Your view is not graded; your reasoning and evidence are.)

### Sources
- Verified: White House, *Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence*
  (December 11, 2025); Indiana House Enrolled Act 1133 (2024), the AI campaign-ad disclaimer law;
  Indiana Department of Education, Digital Learning page linking its AI guidance.
- To confirm in the sources step: where Congress and the court challenges stand as of the week
  of November 16 (the latest reporting found was from September 2026); the count of states with
  school AI guidance (Playlab, September 2026, about 40 plus Puerto Rico); the state AI law count
  (TechPolicy.Press, mid-2026); chatbot minimum-age terms; MagicSchool's teacher-visibility
  setting.
