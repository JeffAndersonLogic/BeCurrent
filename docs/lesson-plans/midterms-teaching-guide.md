# Who Has the Power? — Teaching guide

Generated from scripts/lib/unit-content/midterms.js. Reviewed 2026-10-19.

Civics investigation. Reverse History is optional for this unit.

## Standing schedule

55 minutes before lunch for unit instruction and Canvas filing. Lunch is untimed. After lunch: 10 minutes CNN10 plus 10–15 minutes of daily reporting and News Log. Each OS includes this sequence; its clock counts 80 instructional minutes at the longer setting.

## Goals

- Explain what voters choose in midterms.
- Connect chamber control to governing powers and limits.
- Check claims and separate evidence from prediction.
- Explain verified results with evidence and acknowledge uncertainty.

## Final assessment

Topics 1–4: five practice choices, a brief source note, an application note, and a final explanation. Topic 5: a guided results notebook and one final explanation. Optional planning notes are not required. Political preference is not scored.

Accuracy and reasoning are scored, not political preferences. Students respond privately; the teacher can voice anonymous examples. Typing, dictation, and paper accommodations remain available.

## Topic 1: The Election Nobody Understands

Find out what is on the 2026 ballot, compare the House and Senate, and explain how Congress can change while the president stays in office.

Question: If the president is not on the ballot, why can the midterms change who has power?

### Learning targets

- I can explain what a midterm election changes and what it does not.
- I can distinguish House elections from Senate elections.
- I can explain one way a changed congressional majority could affect government decisions.

### Success criteria

- I can identify congressional offices on the ballot and explain that the presidency is not being elected.
- I can sort at least four of five House/Senate facts correctly and correct my errors.
- I can use one verified fact to explain a possible governing consequence.

### Teacher story

Begin with the missing president on the ballot. Then reveal the people who consider laws, decide spending through legislation, and investigate government. The House is elected all at once every two years; the Senate turns over in parts. Those elections can leave a president facing new partners or opponents. The outcome changes the setting for decisions. It does not guarantee a policy.

### Phase models and teaching decisions

**Opening question**

Model: Yes. Voters can change Congress while the president stays in office. I still need to learn what Congress can do.

Look for: Each student makes a private prediction or selects “not sure.” Notice whether students confuse Congress with the president.

Reteach: If most students think only presidents matter, name one school decision with two decision makers. Then say that national government also divides responsibilities; the analogy does not establish its exact rules.

Cut: Take the private poll without a class debate. Protect every student’s opportunity to predict.

**Today’s direction**

Model: By lunch I will explain who voters choose, how the two chambers differ, and one way that could affect government.

Look for: Students can point to the lesson question and identify the final explanation they will submit.

Reteach: If most students cannot state the purpose, reduce it to: “Who is on the ballot, and why does that matter?” Read it aloud once.

Cut: Do not read every target and criterion aloud. Protect the purpose and show the final-response location.

**Watch / explain**

Model: Congress is the House of Representatives and the Senate. The president is a different office.

Look for: After the selected Congress excerpt, students identify both chambers without including the president.

Reteach: If most students name only the House, draw one bracket labeled Congress around House and Senate. Read the short two-chamber takeaway aloud.

Cut: Stop the Congress excerpt at the planned 3:30 endpoint and omit the remainder of the full episode. Protect the House-plus-Senate distinction.

**Guided practice**

Model: House; Senate; Senate; Both; Both. The House has all 435 voting seats on its regular two-year cycle. Senators have six-year terms.

Look for: Students attempt all five items, use feedback, and correct errors. Specifically check “all 435” versus “all 100.”

Reteach: If most students miss two or more, display two columns: House—435, districts, two years; Senate—100, two per state, six years. Model the first item, then students retry privately.

Cut: Skip optional vocabulary discussion and the federalism extension clip. Protect the five-item attempt and correction.

**Evidence**

Model: The FEC election information establishes the election date. It cannot establish a congressional winner before voting and counting. The chamber explanation helps me understand which offices are involved.

Look for: A named source is attached to a specific fact, and the unknown is a genuine outcome rather than a fact already explained.

Reteach: If most students write a prediction as a fact, make two headings: “The source establishes” and “We cannot know from this.” Place the election date in the first and a future winner in the second.

Cut: Play the 1:09 WFYI clip once; skip replay and the optional federalism clip. Protect time to inspect the source and write the evidence note.

**Apply the idea**

Model: The Indiana Fourth District race chooses a U.S. representative, who can vote on federal spending and serve on committees. It does not elect the president or replace the Senate.

Look for: One plausible institutional change plus one limit. A party name or a promise alone is not a governing explanation.

Reteach: If most students say the House can change every law, trace “House agreement → Senate agreement → presidential action” and ask which steps a House election does not settle.

Cut: Use one consequence and one limit; omit a second hypothetical. Protect the because/could/does-not-guarantee connection.

**Explain / Canvas**

Model: Voters choose members of Congress. A new majority could change leaders and priorities, so the election can change governing power while the president stays. One chamber still cannot ordinarily enact a law alone.

Look for: The checkpoint contains an accurate election fact and a connected consequence. Confirm Canvas’s submitted screen, not merely saved text on BeCurrent.

Reteach: If most students list facts without a connection, supply “Voters choose __. Because __, this could __. It does not guarantee __.” Accept concise completion.

Cut: Omit Push further and extra polishing. Protect the final response and the last five minutes for gathering and Canvas submission.

### Topic rubric

- What voters choose (6): Correctly identify Congress as the House and Senate, distinguish their election cycles, and explain that the presidency is not being elected.
- Fact and source (6): Name a relevant source, accurately use a fact it establishes, and identify something it cannot establish yet.
- Power and limit (6): Connect a changed majority to leadership, priorities, or oversight and explain one change it does not automatically cause.
- Explain your thinking (2): Make the connection understandable in concise writing, dictated text, or the agreed paper response. Spelling and length do not determine this score.

## Topic 2: The Battle for Congress

Follow individual seats to chamber control. Test how a small shift can change leaders, committees, and the agenda without guaranteeing a new law.

Question: How can a few seats change the decisions Congress considers?

### Learning targets

- I can explain how individual elections determine chamber membership.
- I can explain how a majority affects leaders and committees.
- I can distinguish organizing a chamber from passing a law.

### Success criteria

- I can identify the majority in a constructed 435-seat House scenario.
- I can identify two decisions a new majority could influence.
- I can explain one reason chamber control does not guarantee a policy change.

### Teacher story

Move from the ballot to the working room. Separate races produce a chamber. A small difference in seats can decide who organizes that chamber. Leaders and committees can change which issues receive attention before a final vote. Keep the seat model explicitly hypothetical. Then add the limit: one chamber still needs the other chamber and ordinarily presidential action to enact a law.

### Phase models and teaching decisions

**Opening question**

Model: In this constructed House, 218 is more than half of 435. Moving one seat can change which group has the majority.

Look for: Students compare 217 and 218 with the whole chamber rather than treating either number as a real election tally.

Reteach: If most students do not see the difference, split 435 into 217 + 217 + 1. Move the remaining seat visibly to one group.

Cut: Do not discuss actual party seat totals here. Protect the hypothetical-model label.

**Today’s direction**

Model: We will connect one seat to a majority, a majority to leadership, and leadership to priorities—with limits.

Look for: Students can identify the chain they will explain at the end.

Reteach: If most students think the lesson is only arithmetic, ask: “What can the majority decide before a bill gets a final vote?”

Cut: Read the purpose rather than all six target/criterion statements. Protect the distinction between organizing and lawmaking.

**Watch / explain**

Model: A majority helps choose chamber leaders. Leaders influence which issues receive time and attention.

Look for: Students name a leadership job or decision, rather than memorize the historical officeholder shown.

Reteach: If most students only repeat a person’s name, cover the name and label the role: Speaker, leadership, schedule. Ask what the role can influence.

Cut: Use no more than four minutes of the leadership episode. Omit historical personality details and the rest of the episode.

**Guided practice**

Model: Sort: Can influence; Can influence; Not guaranteed; Can influence; Not guaranteed. At 218 in the filled-seat model, Group A can organize the House; Senate agreement is still separate.

Look for: A 217-to-218 slider change is connected to organizing the House. Sort corrections distinguish influence over priorities from guaranteed policy.

Reteach: If most students equate majority with guaranteed law, move the slider across 218 and hold up a separate Senate card that has not changed. Retry items 3 and 5.

Cut: Try only 217 and 218 on the slider; omit additional seat combinations. Protect all five sort attempts and correction.

**Evidence**

Model: The legislative-process reference explains that committees consider bills before floor action. A chair can influence which issues the committee examines, so priorities matter before a final vote.

Look for: A specific committee action, a named source, and an explanation of why the action matters before the whole chamber votes.

Reteach: If most students think committees make every law themselves, place “committee considers” before “full chamber acts.” Have students identify the missing later decision.

Cut: Use no more than four minutes of the committee episode. Omit the optional Alaska report today unless it replaces another activity.

**Apply the idea**

Model: Group A could choose House leaders and prioritize its school-safety proposal. The proposal is not guaranteed to become law: the Senate must agree to the same text and presidential action ordinarily follows.

Look for: The constructed 217-to-218 change leads to a leadership or committee consequence, with another institution identified as a limit.

Reteach: If most students write only “more power,” ask them to replace power with one action: choose leaders, schedule work, or set committee priorities. Add what that action cannot guarantee.

Cut: Use one route through the scenario; omit extra procedural hurdles and Senate threshold extensions. Protect a concrete mechanism and limit.

**Explain / Canvas**

Model: In the constructed House, one seat can turn 217 into a 218-seat majority. That can change leaders and committee priorities. It does not guarantee a new law because the Senate and president still have roles.

Look for: The final response connects seats, organization, and priorities, and states a limit. Verify actual Canvas submission.

Reteach: If most students repeat “218” without explaining why it matters, supply the chain “seat → majority → leadership → priority” and have them explain one link.

Cut: Omit Push further and extra examples. Protect the last five minutes for gathering, pasting, and submitting.

### Topic rubric

- Seats and majority (6): Use the constructed 435-filled-seat model to explain why 218 is a majority. Do not present it as an actual 2026 result.
- Relevant evidence (4): Name a clip or official reference and accurately explain evidence about leaders or committees.
- Influence and limits (8): Explain two decisions a majority can influence and why the other chamber or presidential action still matters for a law.
- Explain your thinking (2): Make the seat-to-power connection understandable. Concise notes, dictated text, and agreed paper work can show the same learning.

## Topic 3: The Power to Stop a President

Investigate laws, spending, oversight, and confirmations. Explain how Congress checks a president and why disagreement can create both accountability and delay.

Question: When does divided power protect the public, and when does it make governing harder?

### Learning targets

- I can explain ways Congress checks presidential power.
- I can explain why one election does not settle every governing decision.
- I can explain a benefit and a difficulty of divided government.

### Success criteria

- I can accurately explain two congressional checks.
- I can explain why one chamber cannot ordinarily enact a law alone.
- I can support an accountability benefit and a governing difficulty with a specific example.

### Teacher story

Follow one lawmaking or spending proposal through the institutions. Then explain why someone might welcome a hearing while someone else worries about delay. Students evaluate governing consequences, not party preference. Distinguish an investigation from a conclusion, and a majority change from removal. Both accountability and governing effectiveness can matter at once.

### Phase models and teaching decisions

**Opening question**

Model: The proposal can be delayed, changed, or stopped. A president cannot ordinarily enact that federal law alone.

Look for: Students recognize that separate institutions can disagree without an election having to be repeated.

Reteach: If most students say the president can simply pass the law, put a House card and Senate card between “proposal” and “presidential action.” Name the agreements still needed.

Cut: Use one private prediction, not a debate about the current president. Protect the institutional question.

**Today’s direction**

Model: We will explain two checks on presidential power and weigh how scrutiny can help while delay can hurt.

Look for: Students can describe both halves of the question: accountability and getting governing work done.

Reteach: If most students hear “checks” only as stopping everything, say: “A hearing may reveal information; agreement may still be needed for a law.”

Cut: Do not explain every constitutional exception in the opener. Protect the question and the two kinds of consequence.

**Watch / explain**

Model: Congress can investigate government actions and decide spending through legislation. The Senate also confirms many presidential nominees.

Look for: Students identify a real check and the institution exercising it. They do not treat a hearing as proof of guilt.

Reteach: If most students mix up the institutions, make three labels: House and Senate—legislation/oversight; Senate—many confirmations; President—sign/veto. Check one example privately.

Cut: Use no more than four minutes of the checks episode. Omit a full tour of every branch’s powers.

**Guided practice**

Model: House and Senate; Senate; President; House and Senate; House and Senate. Overriding a veto requires two-thirds support in each chamber.

Look for: Students attempt and correct the five power classifications. Pay particular attention to confirmation being Senate-only and sign/veto being presidential.

Reteach: If most students miss the same institution, place that sort statement below the correct institution label and explain one example. Students retry it before moving on.

Cut: Skip additional impeachment and Senate procedure examples. Protect correction of the core power classifications.

**Evidence**

Model: Ordinarily the House and Senate must pass the same bill, then the president has a role. Congress can also use oversight to question officials. These are distinct checks, not proof that a particular official did something wrong.

Look for: The evidence note explains two actual congressional checks, names its source, and does not claim one chamber can ordinarily enact a law alone.

Reteach: If most students list branch names without checks, start two clauses: “Congress can ask officials questions through oversight” and “Congress decides legislation.” Then identify which source supports each.

Cut: Use no more than four minutes of the bill episode. Omit extra lawmaking trivia and optional extensions.

**Apply the idea**

Model: The House alone cannot enact the school-lunch plan: the Senate must approve the same text and presidential action follows. A hearing could reveal waste or improve the proposal, but delay could postpone food support for families.

Look for: Both viewpoints receive a fair explanation linked to the constructed spending scenario, not a claim that one party is always good or bad.

Reteach: If most students supply only one side, use two sentence frames: “Scrutiny could help by __” and “Delay could hurt by __.” Let them use the same institutional example.

Cut: Omit a second scenario and whole-class debate. Protect both the benefit and difficulty in a private response.

**Explain / Canvas**

Model: Divided government can do both. A congressional hearing can uncover information about a spending proposal, while disagreement can delay a law needed to fund it. The effect depends on the evidence and decisions, not only the parties involved.

Look for: A supported judgment includes a specific institutional example and both consequences. Verify Canvas’s submitted screen.

Reteach: If most students write only “good and bad,” ask “Which action helps?” and “Which decision might wait?” Help them insert those actions into their response.

Cut: Omit Push further and extra constitutional exceptions. Protect the short explanation and the final five minutes of filing.

### Topic rubric

- Checks and institutions (6): Accurately explain two congressional checks and distinguish a Senate-only confirmation power from powers both chambers exercise.
- Relevant evidence (4): Name a relevant clip or official source and accurately use an institutional fact from it.
- Accountability and governing (8): Use the scenario to explain both an accountability benefit and a governing difficulty, with a concrete mechanism and a limit.
- Explain your thinking (2): Make the reasoning understandable without requiring a long response, public speech, or agreement with a political position.

## Topic 4: The Fight for Your Vote

Investigate campaign framing, check a real claim, and read a poll without treating it as an election result.

Question: What should we check before believing an election message?

### Learning targets

- I can explain how a campaign message directs attention.
- I can check a claim against an independent source.
- I can distinguish a poll, a promise, and a verified result.

### Success criteria

- I can identify a message’s claim, audience, and missing context.
- I can name a second source and explain what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
- I can explain why a poll or promise does not establish what will happen.

### Teacher story

Model one exact claim in a think-aloud. Ask what document or record could test it. The constructed messages show different priorities without inventing real campaign quotations. Then students check one actual message in writing. Evidence may support part of a claim and leave the rest uncertain; that is a useful finding. Apply the same questions to every party.

### Run of show

- 0–3: Opening question (3 minutes)
- 3–5: Today’s direction (2 minutes)
- 5–11: Watch / explain (6 minutes)
- 11–21: Guided practice (10 minutes)
- 21–35: Evidence (14 minutes)
- 35–45: Apply the idea (10 minutes)
- 45–55: Explain / Canvas (10 minutes)
- Lunch: Lunch break (0 minutes)
- After lunch · 10 min: After lunch / CNN10 (10 minutes)
- After lunch · 15 min: After lunch / daily news (15 minutes)

### Phase models and teaching decisions

**Opening question**

Model: A message can select a number or image that draws attention to one concern. I need its source and context before accepting the conclusion.

Look for: Students distinguish a message’s impression from evidence establishing its factual claim.

Reteach: If most students think a number settles the issue, ask what it counts, when it was measured, and what comparison is missing. Do not invent a real campaign quotation.

Cut: Use the private prediction without an open political debate. Protect the source-and-context question.

**Today’s direction**

Model: First notice how a message persuades. Then check one exact factual claim against evidence. Finally say what remains uncertain.

Look for: Students know that identifying framing and verifying a claim are related but separate tasks.

Reteach: If most students say the goal is to pick the better candidate, restate: “We assess a claim and its evidence, not your vote or beliefs.”

Cut: Read the three-part checking routine rather than every target aloud. Protect the difference between claim and proof.

**Watch / explain**

Model: A campaign site can establish what a candidate promises. A relevant independent record is needed to check whether a factual claim is supported.

Look for: Students identify a source that answers the exact question, rather than treating any second website as confirmation.

Reteach: If most students choose two copies of the same message, trace both back to the original. Contrast repeating a claim with checking a relevant record.

Cut: Use the 2:55 WFYI candidate-research clip once. Omit replay and the optional full campaign report.

**Guided practice**

Model: Campaign promise; Poll estimate; Official result; Claim needing checking; Claim needing checking. An estimate from a sample is not a certified election result.

Look for: All five classifications are attempted and corrected. Students distinguish a desired future action from an observation or official outcome.

Reteach: If most students confuse promise and result, contrast “I will seek a law” with “The office certifies these totals.” Have students underline future versus completed action.

Cut: Skip extra examples beyond the five-item sort. Protect feedback and a private retry.

**Evidence**

Model: Constructed Message A emphasizes household costs and addresses voters worried about expenses. It promises support for a bill, but I would need the bill’s details and evidence about its effects to assess the promised savings.

Look for: A constructed-message note identifies claim, audience, and missing information. For the real claim, students can locate the speaker, date, exact claim, and matched independent evidence in the packet.

Reteach: If most students judge only whether they like the message, underline its checkable words. Ask “Which record could support or challenge these words?” Model one match before independent work.

Cut: Use at most three minutes of the media excerpt, or the matched reading instead. Do not add the full campaign report to this phase. Protect the guided evidence inspection.

**Apply the idea**

Model: The rally statement alleges intentional breakage and inability to vote. The technical investigation describes equipment limits and procedures for counting affected ballots. The supplied record does not support the allegation of deliberate interference; its counting evidence contradicts the blanket claim that the printer problem made voting impossible. A technical review and its cited court finding do not narrate every voter’s experience or prove that every election everywhere is free of mistakes.

Look for: The real claim has source/date/link, an independent record relevant to that claim, and a careful conclusion. Unsupported or unresolved is acceptable when justified.

Reteach: If most students copy the claim as proof, place claim and independent record side by side. Model one “supports / contradicts / cannot settle” judgment using the actual packet wording.

Cut: Use the one prepared claim and one matched record. Omit a second claim and optional polling extensions. Protect attribution and the evidence-based conclusion.

**Explain / Canvas**

Model: The poll describes a national registered-voter preference during its field period. Separate district elections award House seats; this survey cannot establish an Indiana district winner, turnout, or November chamber control. A campaign promise also depends on later decisions by the relevant institutions.

Look for: The checkpoint gives a reason for both limits: sampling/reporting for the poll and institutional steps for the promise. Verify actual Canvas submission.

Reteach: If most students say only “it might be wrong,” ask what the poll measured and which institutions still must act on the promise.

Cut: Omit Push further and further poll comparisons. Read only the prepared poll fields needed for the response. Protect five minutes for the explanation and five for Canvas.

### Topic rubric

- Message and framing (4): Identify a message’s claim, intended audience, and missing context without presenting a constructed teaching example as a real quotation.

### Topic rubric

- Accurate results (8): Accurately record House control, Senate control, and one Indiana congressional race as of a stated check time. Attach sources and reporting stages; an honestly unresolved finding can earn full credit.
- Effects and limit (8): Explain two possible governing effects through leadership, committees, oversight, or lawmaking, plus one change the result does not guarantee. Use conditional language when needed.
- Two-source reasoning (6): Identify two sources and explain what each establishes. Distinguish official counts or certification from news race calls and avoid treating repeated reporting as separate proof.

- Remaining uncertainty (4): Name an unresolved result, future policy question, or other genuine uncertainty and explain what additional evidence or event would clarify it.
