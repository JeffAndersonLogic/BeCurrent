The War in Ukraine · Topic 4 · Reverse History stop: 2004–2013

Which Direction?

Before Crimea and Donbas, Ukraine spent years arguing over its political direction. The key is not “East versus West” as two fixed camps. It is a series of contested choices about elections, economic ties, Europe, Russia and security.

Teacher view: One Unit half. Required moves: Orange Revolution → 2008 NATO language → 2013 EU agreement reversal → exit judgment. Avoid treating every Ukrainian voter as permanently pro-Russia or pro-West.

Step 1 · 2004

An election dispute showed that Ukraine's political direction was already contested.

In the 2004 presidential election, Viktor Yanukovych was initially declared the winner amid widespread reports of fraud. Large demonstrations — the Orange Revolution — demanded a fair vote. Ukraine's Supreme Court ordered a repeat runoff, which Viktor Yushchenko won.

What it shows

Ukraine's politics were competitive and unstable.

Power could not simply be assigned from Moscow or assumed by one political bloc. Elections, courts and mass protest all shaped the outcome.

What it does not show

Ukraine was not divided into two permanent countries.

Regional voting patterns mattered, but people changed preferences over time. Treating “east” and “west” as fixed identities hides more than it explains.

Step 2 · 2008

NATO made a promise about the future — but did not make Ukraine a member.

At the April 2008 Bucharest Summit, NATO welcomed Ukraine's membership aspirations and declared that Ukraine and Georgia “will become members of NATO.” But Ukraine did not receive membership then and was not protected by Article 5.

Primary source · NATO

The exact line matters.

The Bucharest declaration said membership would eventually happen and identified a Membership Action Plan as the next step. That created an expectation without creating a defense guarantee.

Security dilemma: Ukraine could see closer NATO ties as protection; Russia could see the same movement as a threat. Those perceptions can coexist without proving that either side's policy was inevitable.

The missing guarantee

Promise of membership ≠ membership.

Ukraine remained outside NATO. That meant the alliance had not committed itself to defend Ukraine under Article 5 if Ukraine were attacked.

Keep this distinction: a future political promise can increase expectations and fears even when the legal security guarantee does not yet exist.

Step 3 · 2010–13

Yanukovych returned to power — then faced a choice between competing relationships.

Viktor Yanukovych won the presidency in 2010. His government negotiated an Association Agreement with the European Union while Ukraine remained economically tied to Russia. In November 2013, the government abruptly suspended preparations to sign the EU agreement. Protests began almost immediately.

EUROMAIDAN

Street pressure returns

Pro-European protests expanded into a broader political movement — the crisis Topic 3 begins with.

The deeper issue was not simply “choose Russia or choose NATO.”

Ukraine was balancing trade, energy, domestic politics, European integration, security cooperation and relations with Russia. Each choice could look like independence to one group and strategic loss to another.
