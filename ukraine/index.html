The War in Ukraine

Russia's full-scale invasion began in 2022, but the conflict did not. Start with the war students can see now, then work backward until today's territorial, security and sovereignty disputes make sense.

The question that holds the unit together

Why did Russia go to war with Ukraine — and why has the war been so difficult to end?

You will not answer that by memorizing a timeline. You will test three explanations against current reporting, maps, decisions and primary-source records.

Before causes, know the ground.

Your earlier Ukraine presentation began with geography. That was the right instinct. The new version keeps that move but separates permanent geography from a frontline that can change.

Five places you need before anything else

Read the war through geography.

KyivUkraine's capital and the center of the national government. It remains a target of Russian long-range attacks.
CrimeaThe Black Sea peninsula Russia seized and annexed in 2014. The United Nations continues to recognize it as part of Ukraine.
Donbas: Donetsk + LuhanskThe eastern region at the center of fighting since 2014 and one of the hardest territorial questions in peace negotiations.
Black Sea + OdesaPorts and shipping connect the war to grain exports, trade, naval power and global food markets.
NATO borderUkraine borders NATO members Poland, Slovakia, Hungary and Romania — but Ukraine itself is not a NATO member.
Do not freeze the frontline into the lesson. Control can change. When we need today's exact line, we will use date-stamped current reporting rather than pretend a permanent course map is live.
Three threads

Every stop backward tests the same three problems.

These are not three separate stories. They overlap, reinforce one another and compete as explanations for why the war began and why a settlement is so difficult.

THREAD 01

Sovereignty & Identity

Ukraine became an independent state in 1991. Russia and Ukraine also share centuries of history that modern leaders interpret very differently.

Who gets to decide what Ukraine is and where Ukraine belongs?
THREAD 02

Security & Alliances

The collapse of the Soviet Union changed borders, military geography and the security system across Europe. NATO then expanded while Ukraine moved closer to Western institutions.

What would make Russia, Ukraine and Europe feel secure?
THREAD 03

Territory & Power

Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk, the Black Sea and other occupied territories are not abstract map labels. They shape military strategy and the terms each side says it could accept.

Who controls the land — and who has the right to decide?

Evidence station

Position is not the same thing as proof.

All governments make arguments about war. Students need to know what a source can tell them, what it cannot prove, and what can be checked independently.

Ukrainian position

Peace cannot simply mean surrender.

On Ukraine's Independence Day, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine wants peace but is not prepared to surrender to Russia. Ukraine has also put ceasefire ideas into U.S.-mediated talks.

Read the Reuters report →
Russian position

Any settlement must reflect the battlefield.

Russian officials say they are willing to hear new proposals, but insist that a settlement reflect what Moscow calls the “realities on the ground.” Russia and Ukraine remain far apart over territory.

What can be verified

Some facts do not depend on accepting either side's motives.

Ukraine is internationally recognized as a sovereign state. Ukraine is not a NATO member. Russia seized Crimea in 2014, and the UN General Assembly affirmed Ukraine's territorial integrity and rejected the Crimean referendum as a basis for changing its status.

Why can't it end?

A ceasefire would have to solve more than shooting.

Topic 1 does not ask students to design a peace treaty. It asks them to see why the basic questions behind one are historically loaded.

PROBLEM 01 · TERRITORY

Where would the border be?

Russia controls Ukrainian territory and claims several Ukrainian regions as Russian. Ukraine rejects the legality of those annexations and still holds territory Russia demands. A ceasefire line is not automatically an agreed border.

PROBLEM 02 · SECURITY

What stops another war?

Ukraine wants durable security guarantees. NATO supports Ukraine but Ukraine is not covered by Article 5. Russia has long objected to Ukraine's possible NATO membership and Western military integration.

PROBLEM 03 · SOVEREIGNTY

Who chooses Ukraine's future?

Ukraine has moved toward NATO and the European Union. Russia has repeatedly treated Ukraine's strategic direction as a matter affecting Russian security and power. The dispute is also about who gets to make that choice.

Which problem looks hardest to solve?

Choose territory, security or sovereignty. Give one reason from today's evidence — not from the history you have not studied yet.
