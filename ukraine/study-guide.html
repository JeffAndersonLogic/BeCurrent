The War in Ukraine · Study Guide

Build the chain, not a pile of dates.

The unit question asks two things: why the war happened and why it has been so difficult to end. Use the six Reverse History stops to build one connected explanation.

Terminal question

Why did Russia go to war with Ukraine — and why has the war been so difficult to end?

A strong answer uses all three threads: sovereignty & identity, security & alliances, and territory & power.

Three threads

Use these as buckets for evidence.

Sovereignty & Identity

Key evidence: 1991 referendum; competing historical narratives; Euromaidan; Ukraine's relationship with EU/NATO.

Question: Who gets to decide what Ukraine is and where it belongs?

Security & Alliances

Key evidence: Soviet collapse; Budapest Memorandum; NATO enlargement; 2008 Bucharest declaration; Russian security demands.

Question: What would make Russia, Ukraine and Europe feel secure?

Territory & Power

Key evidence: Crimea; Donetsk and Luhansk; Black Sea; occupied territory; current peace demands.

Question: Who controls the land — and who has the right to decide?

Documents

Four records you should know what to do with.

Budapest Memorandum · 1994

Know the difference between a security assurance and an automatic military-defense guarantee.

NATO Bucharest Declaration · 2008

Know what NATO promised about Ukraine's future membership — and what Ukraine still did not have.

UN Resolution 68/262 · 2014

Know what it establishes about Crimea and Ukraine's internationally recognized borders.

Putin's 2021 historical-unity argument

Use it as evidence of the Kremlin's historical framing, not as proof that the framing is correct.

Common mistakes

Do not let a shortcut replace the explanation.

“The war started in 2022.”

Too late. The full-scale invasion began then; the armed conflict dates to 2014.

“NATO caused the war.”

Too simple. NATO belongs in the security story, but it does not explain Crimea, Donbas, sovereignty, identity or leadership decisions by itself.

“Russia and Ukraine have always fought.”

False and deterministic. Shared history, imperial rule and Soviet trauma matter because modern actors use and remember them — not because they made war inevitable.

Final preparation

Build a three-turning-point argument.

Pick three turning points from different topics. For each one, be able to say what changed and how that change helps explain either the start or continuation of the war.
