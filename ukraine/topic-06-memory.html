The War in Ukraine · Topic 6 · Final backward stop

Who Owns the Past?

Russia and Ukraine share history. They do not agree on what that history means. The goal today is not to find an ancient event that “caused” the invasion. It is to understand why historical memory gives modern political claims emotional and strategic power.

Teacher view: This is deliberately compressed. Do not let Kyivan Rus become a thousand-year causal chain. Required move: distinguish shared history from modern sovereignty, then return students to the hypothesis they made in Topic 1.

Step 1 · Shared roots

Shared ancestry does not erase modern political boundaries.

Kyiv was a major center of Kyivan Rus, a medieval polity central to the historical traditions of modern Ukraine, Russia and Belarus. Prince Volodymyr/Vladimir's adoption of Christianity in 988 is remembered across those traditions.

A shared historical claim

“We come from the same medieval world.”

That is a meaningful statement about cultural and religious history. It helps explain why symbols, names and places such as Kyiv and Crimea carry enormous weight.

A modern political question

“Who gets to govern Ukraine now?”

That question belongs to the modern era of states, borders and citizens. Shared medieval ancestry does not by itself determine modern sovereignty.

Historical memory can explain why an argument matters without proving the argument is valid.

This distinction is one of the most important source-reading moves in the unit.

Step 2 · Empire & Soviet rule

Ukraine's modern identity was shaped by rule inside larger empires — and by repeated trauma.

Ukrainian lands were divided among neighboring powers for centuries. In the twentieth century, most of Ukraine became part of the Soviet Union. Soviet rule brought industrialization and urbanization, but also coercion, repression, famine, war and policies that shaped language and identity.

1932–33

Holodomor

Stalinist collectivization, grain requisitions and restrictions produced catastrophic famine in Soviet Ukraine. Millions died. In independent Ukraine, the Holodomor became a central national memory of Soviet rule.

1941–45

World War II

Ukraine became a major battlefield under Nazi occupation and Soviet reconquest. Millions of civilians and soldiers died; Jewish communities were devastated in the Holocaust.

POSTWAR USSR

Soviet identity and Russification

Russian was powerful in government, education, media and urban life. Ukrainian language and culture continued, but Soviet authorities periodically treated Ukrainian nationalism as a political threat.

Step 3 · Crimea

Crimea's 1954 transfer later became a political symbol far larger than the administrative act itself.

In February 1954, the Soviet government transferred Crimea oblast from the Russian Soviet republic to the Ukrainian Soviet republic. At the time, both were parts of the USSR; the transfer did not create an international border until the Soviet Union later collapsed.

Historical record

1954 was an internal Soviet transfer.

Contemporary explanations emphasized economic and geographic connections between Crimea and mainland Ukraine. After 1991, the same administrative boundary became part of the international border of independent Ukraine.

Why memory matters

Later political narratives reinterpret earlier events.

Russian narratives have often presented the transfer as an historical mistake. Ukrainian narratives emphasize internationally recognized borders and the legal continuity of the post-Soviet state.

Do not confuse: “this history is politically important” with “this history automatically decides who should control the territory today.”
Step 4 · Historical claim

Putin made history part of Russia's modern case against Ukrainian separation.

In a July 2021 essay, Vladimir Putin argued for the historical unity of Russians and Ukrainians and challenged key parts of Ukraine's national historical narrative. Because it was written by Russia's president shortly before the invasion, the essay is valuable evidence of how the Kremlin framed the relationship.

Primary source · Kremlin

What the essay can show

It records Putin's historical and political argument: Russians and Ukrainians were deeply connected, outside forces had helped divide them, and Ukraine's anti-Russian direction threatened Russia.

What it proves: the historical ideas Putin chose to emphasize. What it does not prove: that his interpretation is the only valid history or that it legally overrides Ukrainian sovereignty.

Corrective evidence · 1991

Modern Ukrainians made a political choice of their own.

Whatever the shared past, 90.3% of participating voters endorsed independence in 1991. Even Donetsk and Luhansk voted overwhelmingly yes. That is evidence about modern political sovereignty that medieval history cannot erase.
