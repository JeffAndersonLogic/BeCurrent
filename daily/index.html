The Desk

The first twenty-five minutes of every class. Watch it, file one dispatch, then read the front page we just built.

What I File My Beat Where to Look
The first 25 minutes

The same three steps, every class.

The Desk runs at the start of the block and then the block moves to the unit. It is deliberately short and deliberately identical every day: a routine you do not have to be told is a routine that leaves room to think about the story instead of the format.

  1. 10 min · Step 1

    Watch

    CNN 10, together, captions on, every class period.

    It is ten minutes, it is built for this room, and it means every student starts the block having seen the same thing. That shared input is what makes the next step possible for a student who had no time, no device, and no news at home last night: the story they need is the one that just played.

  2. 8 min · Step 2

    File

    Everyone files one dispatch. Four short fields, about thirty words.

    Everyone, every day, privately. It is short on purpose: thirty scaffolded words that all thirty students can finish is worth more than a paragraph that twelve of them finish. Type it, dictate it, or hand in a paper card.

  3. 7 min · Step 3

    The Front Page

    The teacher reads three or four dispatches aloud, names removed, and runs one question from Week 01 at them.

    The teacher does the talking. Hearing an expert reason out loud about work the room just produced is the modelling a struggling reader most needs and least often gets, and putting three dispatches on one story side by side is Coverage Compare happening in miniature, every single day.

What you file

One dispatch. About thirty words.

The same four fields every day. They are short, and they are the whole sourcing habit in miniature: who said it, when, what happened, and what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

  1. 1

    Outlet

    Who reported it?

    This came from ___.

  2. 2

    Date

    When?

    It was published on ___.

  3. 3

    What happened

    One sentence. The event, not the reaction.

    The main thing that happened is ___.

  4. 4

    What I would check

    One thing that would change your mind.

    I would want to check ___, because ___.

Start here

Use a story from today’s CNN 10. You already watched it, the sentence stems are on the card, and the outlet and date are in the segment.

Push further

Bring your own story from your beat, or file the same story from two different outlets and say which one you would trust in an argument.

Three ways to file, all equal
  • Type it on this page.
  • Dictate it. Voice typing is a normal way to file, not a special arrangement.
  • Write it on a paper card and hand it in.

House rules

  • Your name is never on the board unless you put it there.

    Dispatches are read aloud with names removed. You can ask to be named.

  • Thirty words is the whole job.

    It is short so that finishing is normal rather than impressive.

  • Talking to one person is optional and never graded.

    It helps some people think. It is not a requirement and nobody is tracking it.

Your lane

Four beats. You file in one of them.

A beat is the lane your dispatch goes in, and it rotates weekly, so roughly a quarter of the room files each lane and the front page has four kinds of story on it. Each beat also carries the question it exists to ask, because finding a story is not the assignment.

Local

Zionsville, Boone County, Indianapolis, Indiana.

Who decided this, and could you go to the meeting where they decide the next one?

The beat with the shortest distance between a story and a student doing something about it. Local government publishes its agendas, its minutes and its budgets, which makes this the easiest beat to check and the one where a student is most likely to find that the reporting left something out.

National

The United States: government, courts, economy, disasters, elections.

What actually changed today, as opposed to what was said today?

The beat most likely to arrive pre-argued. The discipline here is separating the event from the reaction to the event, which is most of what a national news cycle consists of.

International

Anywhere else, including the places currently in the units.

Who is telling us this, and how close are they to it?

The beat where sourcing is hardest and matters most. A story from eight time zones away has usually passed through several hands before it reaches this room, and the number of hands is the story.

Choice

Rotates: Politics, Sports, Entertainment, Pop Culture.

Why is this being covered at all, and who benefits from the coverage?

A sports story is a labour story and a public-money story. An entertainment story is a business story with a press office attached. This beat is not the dessert, it is the beat where students already have expertise, and expertise is what makes bad coverage visible.

The Choice beat rotates

So that all four lanes come up over a month, rather than the loudest one winning every week.

Standing sources

Two you can always start from.

Two rather than fifteen. A long list of sources is a list nobody opens, and these are the two this class already uses. Anything you bring from somewhere else is welcome, and it arrives with the same requirements the dispatch asks for: outlet, date, what happened.

CNN 10

A ten-minute daily news show made for classrooms.

How we use it Watched together at the start of every class period, captions on. It is also the floor for the dispatch: if you have no story of your own, the story you need is the one that just played. It is ten minutes long, which means it is leaving almost everything out, and noticing what it left out is a legitimate dispatch.

The Week

A magazine that summarizes how several outlets covered the same story.

How we use it The browse source when your beat has nothing yet. It is useful here for a specific reason: it shows the same event written up more than one way on a single page, which is the Coverage Compare module happening in the wild.

What you are graded on

The dispatches are practice. The log is the artifact.

Every class One filed dispatch. Typed, dictated, or handwritten. Never spoken to the class, and never shown with your name on it unless you ask for it.
Once a week, in Canvas One News Log a week, submitted through Canvas. It is your five dispatches collected, plus one line about the story that changed your mind.

The dispatches are the practice and the log is the graded artifact. That split is deliberate in both directions. Grading thirty dispatches a day is 5,400 grading events a year and none of them would get read properly. Grading nothing daily means finding out on Friday that Tuesday was lost.
