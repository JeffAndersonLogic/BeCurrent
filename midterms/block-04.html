Who Has the Power? / Topic 4 / Civics

The Fight for Your Vote

Investigate campaign framing, check a real claim, and read a poll without treating it as an election result.

Today’s question

What should we check before believing an election message?

Learning targets and success criteria

Learning targets

  • I can explain how a campaign message directs attention.
  • I can check a claim against an independent source.
  • I can distinguish a poll, a promise, and a verified result.

Success criteria

  • I can identify a message’s claim, audience, and missing context.
  • I can name a second source and explain what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
  • I can explain why a poll or promise does not establish what will happen.

    01Predict

    Notice the message

    Campaigns compete to decide which issues voters notice. Start by considering how the same information can create different impressions.

    Read the Background: What does a campaign want a voter to notice?

    A campaign has limited time to persuade people. It chooses an issue, an image, a number, or a personal story. Those choices direct attention. A message about costs and a message about services can create different priorities in the voter’s mind.

    Begin by identifying the claim in plain language. Then ask who the message appears designed to reach. Understanding a persuasive choice helps you examine the message before deciding whether its factual claim is supported.

    Two messages use the same number. Could they leave different impressions?

    Make a private prediction. It is okay not to know yet.

    02Learn

    Learn how to check it

    Before deciding whether a message is convincing, learn a repeatable checking method. Identify the exact claim, then ask which source could establish it.

    Before the clip: the essential language

    Framing

    Choices that shape how information is presented.

    Corroboration

    Checking information against another source with relevant evidence.

    Poll

    An estimate based on responses from a sample at a particular time.

    Exact claim

    A statement specific enough to check against evidence.

    Independent evidence

    A separate relevant record, not a website repeating the same statement.

    Read the Background: The essentials

    Campaigns persuade through issue choices, numbers, images, and emotional appeals. Those choices are framing. A campaign page is evidence of what a candidate says, but it is not independent proof of the claim or a guarantee the promise can be delivered. Ask who is speaking, what is claimed, what supports it, and what is missing.

    Read the Background: How do you choose a useful checking source?

    Match the source to the claim. A claim about a recorded vote calls for the vote record. A claim about a spending amount calls for the relevant budget or financial document. Reporting can help explain context and point to those records.

    A campaign’s own page can establish what that campaign says. To test the statement, look for evidence independent of the campaign. A second website copying the same statement does not add an independent verification.

    04Apply

    Test a real claim

    Move from the practice messages to a real claim. Trace it to its source and check evidence that can answer the specific question.

    Use the real-claim packet in Chapter 4. Check its exact claim against the independent evidence. Then use the poll card to distinguish measured opinion from an election result.
    Read the Background: What if two sources appear to disagree?

    Check whether they are answering the same question. One may describe a proposal while another describes an existing law. They may use different dates, groups, or measures. First identify the exact point of disagreement.

    Then explain what the evidence supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved. You do not need to force a verdict. A careful unresolved finding is stronger than declaring a claim true or false without evidence that can settle it.

    A real claim: equipment trouble or deliberate interference?

    Guided check / real campaign rhetoric reported October 16, 2026

    They were broken purposely, and you couldn’t vote.

    President Donald Trump, discussing the 2022 Arizona election in a rally excerpt carried by PBS NewsHour on October 16, 2026.

    The report is current campaign coverage; the event being described happened in 2022. We are checking this specific statement, not deciding which party a student should support.

    Independent checking record

    What the technical review found

    Tests linked the printing failures to a difficult combination of ballot size, heavier paper, and printer capability. The investigators tested 9,100 ballots. This supplies an equipment explanation rather than evidence that someone deliberately broke machines.

    McGregor report, printed pages 2–3 / Summary

    What happened to affected ballots

    The report’s page 2 footnote explains that unreadable ballots could be sent for central counting or copied onto readable ballots by a bipartisan board and counted. It cites court proceedings concerning those votes.

    McGregor report, printed page 2 / footnote 1

    Keep the conclusion narrow

    Printer failures and delays were real. Those problems alone do not establish deliberate interference or that ballots could not be counted. This short record does not describe every voter’s individual experience.

    05File

    Explain what remains uncertain

    A useful check may reveal uncertainty. Explain what your evidence establishes and why a poll or promise cannot settle an election or a future policy decision.

    Read the Background: Why does a favorable poll fail to guarantee a win?

    A poll estimates opinion from a sample at a particular time. Who was asked, the wording, timing, and uncertainty affect what the estimate can tell us. The people who answer are not the complete future electorate.

    A promise has a different limit: even a winning candidate may need cooperation from other people and institutions. Keep three questions separate: what people currently say, who wins the election, and what government later does.

    How this investigation is assessed / 5 points

    Political preferences are not scored. Short writing, dictated text, and agreed paper responses can demonstrate the same learning.

    • Message and framing. Identify a message’s claim, intended audience, and missing context without presenting a constructed teaching example as a real quotation.
    • Check the real claim. Record the actual claim and source/date/link, name independent evidence, and explain exactly what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
    • Polls, promises, uncertainty. Explain why a sample-based poll does not establish an election winner and why a campaign promise does not guarantee a policy change.
    • Explain your thinking. Make the evidence-to-conclusion connection understandable. Political preference, response length, and public speaking are not scored.

    Two or three clear sentences are enough.

    Check your response:
    • Use the poll’s dates and population.
    • Separate an estimate from a verified result.
    • State a condition on delivering a promise.
    Start here

    This poll estimates ____ among ____ at ____. It cannot establish ____. A promise also depends on ____.
