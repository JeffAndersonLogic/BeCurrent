Learning targets and success criteria

Learning targets

  • I can explain how a campaign message directs attention.
  • I can check a claim against an independent source.
  • I can distinguish a poll, a promise, and a verified result.

Success criteria

  • I can identify a message’s claim, audience, and missing context.
  • I can name a second source and explain what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
  • I can explain why a poll or promise does not establish what will happen.

    READ / What is a political campaign?

    Political campaigns try to persuade voters to support a candidate or issue. Because ads are short, campaigns choose which images, statistics, and personal stories will receive attention.

    That choice is called framing. A statement may contain an accurate number or quotation but leave out another fact that changes how we understand it. Our job is to identify the message and check it without judging someone’s political preference.

    READ / How do campaigns use persuasion?

    Campaign messages may use fear, hope, humor, personal experiences, selective facts, and emotional images. The goal is to help one message stick with the viewer.

    A campaign ad is useful evidence of what its sponsors say. It is not neutral proof. An independent record, such as an official voting record, a budget estimate, or a reliable fact-check, can help test a specific statement.

    04Check evidence

    Check the receipts

    Use the government records and a FactCheck.org investigation to identify what the advertisements establish and what they leave out.

    READ / How to compare conflicting claims

    A law can fund one program and also reduce other spending. These things can both be true. Check who gets the money, what the money pays for, and what years each number covers.

    A risk estimate is not a confirmed future event. If the record cannot show how many hospitals will close, say so. A responsible conclusion identifies both what is supported and what remains uncertain.

    READ / THE EVIDENCE / AFTER QUESTION 6

    What does the full record establish?

    Record 1 · New rural-health money. The federal government created the Rural Health Transformation Program with $50 billion to be distributed over five fiscal years (2026–2030). These funds support approved rural-health projects; they do not simply replace every dollar of hospital operating revenue.

    Medicaid.gov / Rural Health Transformation Program ↗

    Record 2 · Medicaid spending changes. The Congressional Budget Office projected roughly $900 billion in federal Medicaid spending reductions over ten years under the 2025 law. That is a national projection, with a different purpose and time span from the five-year fund, not a count of rural hospitals that will close.

    Congressional Budget Office / original estimates ↗

    Record 3 · Hospital closure claims. FactCheck.org found the Iowa ad cited an earlier list of financially at-risk hospitals. The list was not a prediction of how many would close because of the new law.

    FactCheck.org / September 17, 2026 investigation ↗

    Evidence limit: These sources establish new investment and projected spending reductions. Neither ad alone tells the full story, and the evidence does not establish a specific number of future closures. These ads address different elections and candidates but the same law.

    READ / A promise and a poll are not election results

    A poll asks a sample of people what they think at one point in time. The responses are estimates, not votes counted or certified on Election Day. A national survey also cannot tell us exactly how one congressional district will vote.

    A campaign promise tells us what a politician wants to do, not what they already accomplished. A new federal law normally requires action from Congress and the president. We need different evidence for a promise, a poll, a recorded vote, and an official result.
