2. Can the president actually do that?

Read the notes about congressional checks. Open the power cards, complete and correct the five practice choices, then answer Questions 3 and 4.

Read the notes: How does Congress check the president?

  • Lawmaking: Congress considers and passes laws.
  • Spending: Congress decides federal spending through laws.
  • Oversight: hearings and investigations ask officials to explain decisions.
  • Confirmations: the Senate confirms many presidential nominees.

Key idea: Name the specific check: laws, spending, oversight, or confirmation.

Read the notes: What can oversight do, and what can’t it do?

  • Oversight means Congress examining how government uses its authority.
  • Hearings can establish facts and bring out information.
  • An investigation does not prove wrongdoing by itself.
  • A new majority does not remove a president. Impeachment and conviction are separate processes.

Key idea: Oversight gathers evidence. It is not proof.

The ordinary route

House agreement → Senate agreement on the same text → presidential action. A veto override requires two-thirds in each chamber.

Two checks

Both chambers conduct oversight. The Senate alone confirms many presidential nominees.

The power panel

One president. Several decision-makers.

3. Try to get lunch funded

Read the bill-route notes, then try the school-lunch funding lab. Record two outcomes in Question 5. Use the official sources for Question 6.

Read the notes: How does a bill become a law?

  • The House and the Senate must ordinarily pass the same bill.
  • If they pass different versions, the differences must be resolved.
  • Then the president can sign the bill or veto it.
  • Congress can override a veto with two-thirds support in each chamber.

Key idea: One chamber cannot ordinarily make a law alone.

Bill lab / paper route

Constructed school-lunch proposal. Trace a blocked route and a route that makes the bill law. Use these rules:

  • House approval alone is insufficient.
  • Both chambers must ordinarily agree to the same text.
  • If both chambers pass the same bill and the president signs it, it becomes law.
  • A veto blocks the bill unless two-thirds in each chamber override it.

Follow the same proposal through the institutions

The school-lunch proposal is a constructed example. Ordinarily, both chambers must approve the same bill before it goes to the president. A House committee hearing is neither Senate approval nor a new law. Use this route when judging scrutiny and delay.

4. Who pays for the delay?

Read the scrutiny-and-delay notes. Think about families waiting for school-lunch support, then answer Question 7 using three short bullets.

Read the notes: How can a check be useful and frustrating?

  • Scrutiny can uncover problems, such as waste, and improve a proposal.
  • Delay can create costs for people waiting for a service.
  • Divided government can bring accountability and compromise, or delay and stalemate.
  • None of these follows automatically from a party label.

Key idea: Judge a check by its evidence: what it reveals, how long it delays, and who is affected.

Practice scenario (made up for class, not a real 2026 bill): The president proposes more school-lunch funding. The House committee questions its cost. Some families want careful scrutiny; others need the service quickly. The Senate has not agreed to the proposal.
