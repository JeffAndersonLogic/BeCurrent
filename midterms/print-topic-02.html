The Battle for Congress

How can a few seats change the decisions Congress considers?

Learning targets

  • I can explain how individual elections determine chamber membership.
  • I can explain how a majority affects leaders and committees.
  • I can distinguish organizing a chamber from passing a law.

Success criteria

  • I can identify the majority in a constructed 435-seat House scenario.
  • I can identify two decisions a new majority could influence.
  • I can explain one reason chamber control does not guarantee a policy change.

Background

Each House district elects a representative. In a simplified House with 435 filled voting seats and two groups, 218 is more than half. In real votes, vacancies and attendance can matter. A majority generally organizes the chamber, chooses leaders, and controls committee leadership. That affects which issues receive time and attention.

Committees are smaller working groups. They study issues, question officials, and consider bills before the full chamber acts. A chair can influence what the committee examines. A proposal can face obstacles before any final vote. Prioritizing an issue is real power, even when leaders cannot guarantee a law.

The Senate has 100 seats. A group with 51 has an outright majority. A 50–50 split also requires attention to the vice president’s tie-breaking role and chamber organization. Some Senate procedures create hurdles beyond a simple majority. Chamber control does not mean enough votes for every action. House races are by district; Senate races are statewide.

Application

Start here: Group A could organize ____, but a law still needs ____.

Start here: A small seat change could ____. That does not guarantee ____.
