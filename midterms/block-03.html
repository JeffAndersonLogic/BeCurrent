Read the background

Congress considers legislation and decides federal spending through laws. It also conducts oversight: checking government actions through hearings and investigations, among other tools. The Senate confirms many presidential appointments. Those powers matter whether leaders share a party or disagree.

Evergreen explanation. Names, amounts, and earlier election examples in this clip are dated examples, not 2026 results.

Alternate playback ↗

Pause after presidential action; trace the same text through both chambers.

Read the background

Ordinarily, the House and Senate pass the same bill before it goes to the president. The president can sign it or take another constitutionally allowed action. A veto rejects the bill. Congress can override a veto with two-thirds support in each chamber. Rules about inaction and adjournment add details; the key point is that one chamber cannot usually enact a law alone.

Read the background

Divided government can bring stronger scrutiny and pressure to compromise. It can also bring delay or stalemate. Neither follows automatically from a party label. An investigation does not establish wrongdoing by itself. Changing a majority does not remove a president: impeachment and conviction are separate constitutional processes.
