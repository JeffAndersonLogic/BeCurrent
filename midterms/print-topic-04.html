The Fight for Your Vote

What should we check before believing an election message?

Learning targets

  • I can explain how a campaign message directs attention.
  • I can check a claim against an independent source.
  • I can distinguish a poll, a promise, and a verified result.

Success criteria

  • I can identify a message’s claim, audience, and missing context.
  • I can name a second source and explain what it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved.
  • I can explain why a poll or promise does not establish what will happen.

01 / Commit before you reveal

Your first impression

Two short bullets are enough. Do not open the evidence reveal until you make your first judgment.

02 / REVEAL THE EVIDENCE · Open only after your first impression

Document 1: A real new investment

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services describes a $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program spread over five fiscal years, 2026–2030. States can use approved funds for specific rural-health improvements. That is not the same as replacing all hospital operating revenue.

Primary record: Medicaid.gov / Program structure ↗

Document 2: A projected spending reduction

The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office estimates the law will reduce federal Medicaid outlays by about $915 billion nationally during 2025–2034, compared with its baseline. This is a projection, not an observed count of hospital closures. The Medicaid figure covers a different period and scope from the rural-health program.

Primary record: CBO supplemental cost estimate / page 1 ↗

Document 3: What about 20 hospitals?

The 20 Iowa hospitals were identified using earlier financial-risk data, before the federal law passed. Being at risk is not proof that 20 hospitals will close because of this law. Financial risk and a confirmed closure are different claims.

Independent analysis: FactCheck.org / September 17, 2026 ↗

Important: The ads concern different candidates and states, but describe the same federal law. A $50 billion program and a roughly $915 billion Medicaid projection cannot simply be subtracted: different programs, time periods, and populations are involved. The evidence supports both a new program and projected reductions, not a guaranteed outcome for any particular hospital.

Two or three sentences. Cite Medicaid.gov, CBO, or FactCheck.org by name and explain what the source actually establishes.

If the election were held then, which U.S. House candidate in their district would respondents choose? Candidate names and parties were shown in most districts.

  • Democratic candidate: 44%
  • Republican candidate: 36%
  • Another candidate: 5%
  • Not sure: 15%

The registered-voter sample has a margin of sampling error of ±1.8 percentage points at 95% confidence. Wording, nonresponse, and later turnout can add uncertainty. This is not an error band for the number of seats won.

How this investigation is assessed / 5 points

Political preferences are not scored. Short writing, dictated text, and agreed paper responses can demonstrate the same learning.

  • Before evidence. Identify one initial impression or exact claim in each actual 2026 ad.
  • Independent check. Name Medicaid.gov, CBO, or FactCheck.org and accurately state a finding and its limits.
  • After evidence. Compare what each ad emphasizes, what it omits, and whether the original impression changed or held.
  • Polls and promises. Explain why a sampled poll and a campaign promise are not election results or guaranteed policy outcomes.
