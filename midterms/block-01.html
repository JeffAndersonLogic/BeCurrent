Teacher notes, learning targets, and the run of show

90-minute block

0–25: The Desk · 25–31: Hook · 31–43: Congress video · 43–55: Chamber sort · 55–67: PBS reporting · 67–80: Power scenario · 80–90: Exit and Canvas.

Students will be able to

  • Explain what a midterm election changes.
  • Distinguish the House and Senate.
  • Describe one consequence of a changed congressional majority.

Evidence of success

  • Identify the federal office that is not on the ballot.
  • Sort House/Senate facts accurately.
  • Use one fact to explain why the election matters.

This unit examines institutions and evidence, not which candidate or party students should support. Videos are individually linked rather than embedded.

THE INVESTIGATION

Follow the story.

Watch together. Investigate in short steps. Your work saves on this Chromebook until you paste it into Canvas.

0 of 4 investigation parts started

01 / THE OPENING QUESTION

Who makes federal laws?

Make a prediction before the video. It's okay not to know yet.

Watch for this: Congress has two chambers. They both vote on federal legislation.

02 / TWO CHAMBERS

House, Senate, or both?

Sort five statements about Congress. You can try again if you need to.

Start here / House and Senate in 30 seconds

House: 435 voting seats, with elections every two years. A state's population determines its House seats.

Senate: 100 seats, two per state. Six-year terms, with about one-third up every two years.

Both: Vote on legislation and have majority and minority leaders.

Practice only. No grade for mistakes.

Talk with a partner: What are voters choosing in 2026? What are they not choosing?

Go deeper / Why different election cycles?

Why do all House seats face voters every two years while only a third of the Senate usually does? What might each design make possible?

03 / THE REAL-WORLD STORY

What did the news actually prove?

The PBS report covers 2026 primary elections. What happened, and what is still undecided?

One important difference: A primary decides who advances in a party's nomination process. It does not decide who wins every race in November.
Start here / Use this sentence frame

“The report shows ______ happened during a primary. It does not prove ______ will happen in November.”

Go deeper / Evaluate the source

What kinds of evidence appear: election results, campaign statements, or analysis? How does that affect your conclusion?

04 / APPLY THE IDEA

What if the House changes hands?

Imagine a different party controls the House after the election, but the president stays in office. What could change?

Hypothetical, not a prediction: New House leaders might change legislative priorities, committee chairs, or oversight. Changing the House alone would not remove the president or automatically pass new laws.
Start here / Build a two-part answer

“The new House majority might ______. The president would still ______.”

Go deeper / Why isn't one chamber enough?

Explain why the House cannot ordinarily turn a bill into federal law by itself. What roles do the Senate and president play?

05 / YOUR TAKEAWAY

Why does this election matter?

In two or three sentences, answer the question using one verified fact.

Start here / Try this frame

“The midterms matter because voters choose ______. If a majority changes, ______ could change, even though ______ stays the same.”

Go deeper / Show a limit

Explain the difference between changing political leadership and actually passing a new federal law.
