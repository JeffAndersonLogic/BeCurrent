Learning targets

  • Explain what a midterm election changes.
  • Distinguish the House and Senate.
  • Explain one effect of a different congressional majority.

Success criteria

  • Identify which federal office is not on the ballot.
  • Sort House/Senate facts accurately.
  • Use evidence to explain why the election matters.

Your job today

Follow the story. Show what you know.

Watch the videos with your class, then use these short activities. All answers save on this Chromebook. You will gather your work and paste it into Canvas at the end.

0 of 4 investigation parts started

Step 1 / Predict

Who makes federal laws?

Before the videos, make a prediction. It is okay not to know yet.

Watch for: Congress has two chambers. Most proposed federal laws require passage in both chambers before going to the president.

Step 2 / Congress decoded

House, Senate, or both?

Choose which chamber each fact describes. Check your sort when you finish.

Start here: The two-chamber cheat sheet

House: 435 voting seats. Elections every two years. Seats are based on state populations.

Senate: 100 seats. Two senators per state. Six-year terms, with about one-third elected every two years.

Both: Make up Congress and consider federal laws. Each has party leadership.

This is practice, not a grade.

Make the connection

Turn and talk: In 2026, what are Americans choosing? What federal office are they not choosing? Explain the difference to a partner.

Push further: Why design different election cycles?

Consider why voters choose the entire House every two years but only one of three Senate classes in each regular election. What kinds of stability or change might each structure create?

Step 3 / Real news in 2026

What did the news report actually establish?

After the PBS NewsHour clip, explain the difference between what happened in the primaries and what is still undecided in the November general election.

Remember: A primary can determine which candidates advance. It does not settle every November contest.
Start here: Sentence starters

“The PBS report shows that ______ happened during the primary. It does not prove that ______ will happen in November.”

Push further: Source check

Does the reporter cite candidates, official results, analysts, or something else? How does the source affect what we can reasonably conclude?

Step 4 / The decision

One branch stays. Another changes.

Hypothetical situation: Imagine that a different political party wins control of the House, while the president remains in office. Choose one institutional change to explain.

Possible effects: The House could choose different leaders, select new committee chairs, change legislative priorities, or approach oversight differently. A different House majority would not, by itself, remove the president or guarantee any new law.
Start here: Build a two-part answer

1. “The new House majority might ______.” 2. “The president would still ______.” Use a real power of Congress from the lesson.

Push further: Consider a limit

Explain why action by the House alone does not necessarily become federal law. Consider the Senate and the president.

Step 5 / The takeaway

Why does the election matter?

Answer our big question in two or three sentences. Include at least one verified fact from a video or the House/Senate explanation.

Start here: Two-sentence answer

“The midterms matter because voters choose ______. If the majority changes, ______ could change, although ______ would stay the same.”

Push further: Be precise

Distinguish a change in leadership from a law being passed. What other institutions are involved in turning a proposal into law?
