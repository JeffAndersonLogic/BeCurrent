The Whole Unit, On One Page

6 topics, 26 words you have to be able to use, and the cases you should be able to name.

How to use this

Work down it topic by topic. Anything you cannot say out loud, go back to that topic’s Brief and reread the part it came from. This page is an index of what you were taught, not a replacement for it, and a word you can only recognize is a word you cannot use.

What is on the assessment

All 6 topics. Nothing on it asks you whether social media is good or bad, and nothing asks you to take a side on the question below. It asks how the thing works, what the evidence says, and what the argument about it is made of.

The question the unit ends on. Who should get to decide what a platform is allowed to do to your attention: you, the company, or the government? You are not graded on which side you land. You are graded on whether you can state the strongest version of the argument against you, and on whether you can name the tradeoff in your own position and not only in somebody else’s.

Topic 1 · done on paper

Where the Money Comes From

The big idea. Every app you opened today was free, and not one of those companies is a charity. Today you build the chain backwards: from you opening an app to somebody getting paid, one box and one arrow at a time. You will do it on paper, because a device turns a trace into copy-paste from a search result. Then you walk the room and read four other chains, which is where you find out what you left out.

You have got it when you can

  • Cause and EffectI can draw a chain with at least four boxes in it and no gap I cannot explain out loud.
  • Cause and EffectI can name the thing being sold, who is buying it, and who is being sold.
  • Generalizing from EvidenceI can name one box I got wrong or left out after reading four other chains, and say which chain changed my mind.

The moves to have ready

The chain
You open a free app, the app holds your attention, an advertiser pays to be put in front of it. Four boxes minimum, and no gap you cannot explain out loud.
Who is the customer
The advertiser, because the advertiser is the one who hands over money. Not you. You are what is being sold access to.

Topic 2 · done on paper

Somebody Made a Film About This

The big idea. Somebody made a film about the thing you traced last class. We are going to watch it. Your job is not to agree with it, and it is not to disagree with it either. Your job is to do to the film exactly what you did to your app: take it apart, find out how it works, and see where the money is. Every film was made by somebody, for a reason, with money from somewhere.

You have got it when you can

  • SourcingI can say who made the film and point to one thing in it that tells me what it wants me to believe.
  • Fallacies and PropagandaI can point at one specific scene and name the technique it uses on me.
  • Cause and EffectI can state one claim the film makes that I would want to check, and say what evidence would settle it.

The moves to have ready

Who made it, and what do they gain
The first question about any film, and the same question you ask of a post. A documentary is a genre, not a standard of proof.
Proves against suggests
A dramatized scene performed by actors illustrates a claim. It does not establish it. Separating those two is the whole job.

Topic 3

Inside the Algorithm Box

The big idea. The app counts what you do, sorts you in with people who behave like you, and shows you what held them. Then your reaction becomes the next count. Somebody chose which number that loop is pushing on.

You have got it when you can

  • Cause and EffectI can list three engagement signals an app collects without me sending it anything.
  • Cause and EffectI can explain, out loud, why two people who open the same app at the same second see different things.
  • HypothesesI can name a number an app could optimize for, and say what would go wrong with that choice.

Words you have to be able to use

ranking
Ranking means putting things in an order that somebody chose.
engagement signal
An engagement signal is anything you do that the app can count.
feedback loop
A feedback loop is when the result of something becomes the cause of the next thing.
optimize
To optimize means to push a number as high as it will go.
A/B test
Companies find out which choice wins by running an A/B test: show version A to some users and version B to others, then keep whichever one performed better.
chronological
A chronological feed is the other option: newest first, oldest last, no decisions.

Names, dates and cases

Facebook News Feed, 2006
Launched mostly in time order. Feeds were not born ranked.
Instagram switches to ranking, 2016
The company said people were missing about 70 percent of their feed. A real problem, and ranking is a real solution to it. It also made more money, and when two causes point the same way it is hard to say which did the work.
Twitter adds the time-order switch back, 2018
Still there. Ranking did not fully win, which is why the question is why the ranked version is the default rather than why ranking won.
Instagram Following and Favorites, 2022
Both chronological. Same point: the choice is still being made.
The standard for a complete answer. A complete answer in this class explains the mechanism, not just the outcome. "The app knows what I like" is an outcome. "The app counted how long I watched, found people with similar counts, and showed me what held them" is a mechanism.

Do not oversimplify this. Ranked feeds were not inevitable, and some of them came back.

Check yourself

  1. Cause and EffectName three things an app can count about you when you have not posted, liked, or commented on anything.
  2. Cause and EffectExplain the feedback loop in your own words. Why does the app get better at holding your attention the longer you use it?
  3. HypothesesEvery ranking system pushes on a number that a person chose. If you ran the app, what number would you pick, and what would go wrong with your choice?

These are the questions from this topic's Brief. If one of them stops you, the answer is in that reading rather than here, and going back to it is the point.

Topic 4

The Impact and the Digital Footprint

The big idea. The good effects and the bad effects are both real, they land unevenly, and how big an effect is matters as much as whether it exists. The record is a different kind of question: it is longer than what you posted, parts of it are not yours to delete, and people who decide things about you can read some of it.

You have got it when you can

  • Cause and EffectI can state a claim about social media and mental health, and say whether the evidence behind it shows a cause or only a pattern.
  • CorroborationI can name two things I would want to know about a study before I believed its headline.
  • Generalizing from EvidenceI can name three places a piece of my footprint sits that I cannot delete myself.

Words you have to be able to use

correlation
Correlation means two things move together.
causation
Causation means one makes the other happen.
effect size
The second idea you need is effect size: not whether an effect exists, but how big it is.
social comparison
Social comparison is the name for measuring yourself against other people, and it is normal.
digital footprint
Your digital footprint is the trail of data your activity leaves behind.
data broker
Then there are data broker companies, whose whole business is buying records about people from many sources, combining them into one profile, and selling it.
two-factor authentication
Something you know plus something you have, usually a code on your phone.

Names, dates and cases

Orben and Przybylski, 2019, Nature Human Behaviour
Ran the numbers across large datasets and found the association between screen use and teenage wellbeing was tiny, about the size of associations nobody worries about. A claim about effect size, not about whether an effect exists.
Haidt, The Anxious Generation, 2024
Argues from timing: teenage anxiety and depression rose sharply around 2012, when smartphones and social media became close to universal.
Odgers reviews it in Nature, 2024
Argues the evidence does not support a causal claim that strong, and that blaming phones pulls attention off causes that are better established. Two serious people, opposite conclusions, neither one lying.
Haugen and the Wall Street Journal, September 2021
Internal Facebook research, leaked by a former employee, in which the company's own researchers found Instagram made body image worse for some teenage girls. Strong because it is the company studying itself. Narrow because it is about a subset of users.
Harvard withdraws offers, June 2017
At least ten incoming students, over messages posted in a private group chat. Private group, real consequence, nobody hacked anything.
The right to be forgotten, May 2014
The European Court of Justice ruled people can require search engines to remove certain results about them. Permanent is a policy choice, not a law of physics.
The standard for a complete answer. A complete answer in this class names the mechanism and the size. "It causes depression" has neither. "Comparing against curated images lowers self-esteem, and the average effect is small but much larger for some people" has both.

Do not oversimplify this. Permanent is a policy choice, and the policy is still moving.

Check yourself

  1. Cause and EffectPick one negative effect from Part Two. Name the mechanism: step by step, what connects using the app to feeling worse?
  2. CorroborationTwo researchers study the same teenagers and reach opposite conclusions. Give two reasons that can happen without either of them lying.
  3. Generalizing from EvidenceName three pieces of your digital footprint that sit somewhere you cannot delete. For one of them, say what somebody could decide about you from it.

These are the questions from this topic's Brief. If one of them stops you, the answer is in that reading rather than here, and going back to it is the point.

Topic 5

How a Lie Travels

The big idea. False stories spread because they are new and because they produce strong emotions, and the best available evidence says that people, not bots, do most of the spreading. Checking a claim takes about two minutes, and a real check names what you looked at. In an election every tactic in this reading appears at once, and how much any of it changes a result is still argued about.

You have got it when you can

  • Fallacies and PropagandaI can name the emotion a post is aiming for, and explain why that emotion helps it spread.
  • SourcingI can trace a claim back to its earliest source, or say honestly that I looked and could not find one.
  • CorroborationI can explain the difference between a claim that is false and a claim that is true but framed to mislead.

Words you have to be able to use

misinformation
Misinformation is false information shared by someone who believes it is true.
disinformation
Disinformation is false information shared by someone who knows it is false.
clickbait
A headline written to get a click rather than to tell you what happened.
deepfake
Video or audio of a real person, made by software, saying something they never said.
bot
A bot is an account that is automated rather than run by a person.
lateral reading
The most useful habit is lateral reading. Instead of studying the page you landed on more and more closely, you leave it.
echo chamber
This is the idea that ranking narrows what a person sees until they mostly encounter people who already agree with them, so opposing arguments arrive only in weak forms.
microtargeting
Campaigns pay to show messages to narrow groups of voters, selected by attribute.

Names, dates and cases

The Associated Press account is hacked, April 2013
One false sentence about explosions at the White House, from an account readers had good reason to trust. The stock market dropped within seconds and recovered within minutes.
Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, 2018, Science
About 126,000 rumors on Twitter over eleven years. False stories reached more people, faster. Then they removed every automated account and the result did not change: people, not bots, do most of the spreading. Their explanation was novelty and emotion.
Peele and BuzzFeed, April 2018
A video of Barack Obama that Obama had nothing to do with, made specifically to show people it was possible.
Veles, North Macedonia, 2016
BuzzFeed News traced false American political stories to a group of sites run by young people who had found the traffic paid well. The motive was money, not politics, which surprises most people.
Pizzagate, December 2016
A false claim with no evidence behind it moved a man to drive to a restaurant in another state and fire a rifle inside it.
The Stanford fact-checker study
Professional fact-checkers left the page almost immediately. Students stayed and studied the design, the logo and the About section, all of which the page controls.
The Internet Research Agency indictment, February 2018
Thirteen Russian nationals, for running accounts that posed as Americans in 2016. Read the goal carefully: much of it aimed to widen existing divisions rather than to argue for one candidate.
Cambridge Analytica, 2018
Profile data on tens of millions of Facebook users, collected through a quiz app and used for political targeting. Facebook agreed to a five billion dollar penalty from the Federal Trade Commission in 2019.
Bakshy, Messing and Adamic, 2015, Science
On Facebook, individual choices about what to click mattered more than the ranking did for how much opposing content people actually read. The authors worked for Facebook, which is a reason to read it carefully rather than to throw it out.
The standard for a complete answer. "I do not believe it" is not a check. A check names what you looked at. "Two outlets report this, and both cite the same anonymous source" is a check. It can end in uncertainty and still be finished work.

Do not oversimplify this. The echo chamber may be the part of this that is wrong.

Check yourself

  1. Fallacies and PropagandaDescribe one post you saw this week that gave you a strong emotional reaction. Name the emotion, then explain how that emotion made people more likely to share the post.
  2. SourcingChoose one claim you are unsure about and check it using lateral reading. Write down what you did: who published it, when it was published, and whether any other source reports it.
  3. Generalizing from EvidenceChoose one item from Part Five. Explain, in three steps, how it could change the result of an election.

These are the questions from this topic's Brief. If one of them stops you, the answer is in that reading rather than here, and going back to it is the point.

Topic 6

Who Gets to Decide?

The big idea. There are three answers, and each one has a real case and a real tradeoff. This argument has happened four times before, about four different kinds of media, and it usually ended in self-regulation instead of a new law. Your job today is not to win the argument. Your job is to state the other side well enough that somebody who holds it would say you got it right.

You have got it when you can

  • Synthesizing PatternsI can state the position I did not choose well enough that somebody who holds it would say I got it right.
  • Synthesizing PatternsI can name the tradeoff in the position I actually hold, not just the one I argued against.
  • HypothesesI can name what would change my mind, specifically enough that somebody could go looking for it.

Words you have to be able to use

regulation
Regulation means the government requires a company to follow certain rules.
self-regulation
When a company makes its own rules instead of waiting for government rules, that is called self-regulation. Self-regulation is a real force, not just words: companies do change their products, sometimes because users complain and sometimes because bad press costs them money.
precedent
A precedent is an earlier case that people point to when they are deciding a new one.
tradeoff
A tradeoff is something you give up in order to get something else.
unintended consequence
An unintended consequence is a result that nobody wanted or predicted.

Names, dates and cases

Comic books, 1954
A Senate hearing and a new industry code. Later research showed the studies behind the alarm were seriously flawed.
Television violence, 1970s
Hearings across the whole decade.
Music lyrics, 1985
Senate hearings, and the parental advisory sticker still on album covers.
Video games, 1993
Hearings, and the rating system still on every game box. The research is still argued about today.
What all four have in common
None of them ended with a new law. Each ended with the industry writing its own rules, only after the government threatened to step in.
Section 230, 1996
One sentence: a platform is usually not treated as the publisher of what its users post. You can still be held responsible for what you post; the platform usually cannot.
The Supreme Court, 1997
Struck down the rest of the law Section 230 was part of, for violating free speech. Only that one sentence survived, and people are still fighting over it.
The standard for a complete answer. A tradeoff is something you give up in order to get something else. Every answer here has one. An answer with no tradeoff in it is not finished.

Do not oversimplify this. The rules we have now were chosen, and one of them almost disappeared.

Check yourself

  1. Synthesizing PatternsChoose the position you do not agree with. Write the strongest case for it, in your own words.
  2. Generalizing from EvidenceChoose one earlier argument: comic books, television, music lyrics, or video games. Describe what happened, then name one way social media is different from that medium.
  3. Generalizing from EvidenceWhat evidence would change your mind about who should decide? Be specific enough that somebody could go looking for it.

These are the questions from this topic's Brief. If one of them stops you, the answer is in that reading rather than here, and going back to it is the point.

BeReady: what the whole unit was for

An app counts what you do, sorts you in with people who behave like you, and shows you what held them, all to move a number somebody chose. The effects of that are real, they land unevenly, and how big an effect is matters as much as whether it exists. False stories travel on novelty and feeling, and people do most of the carrying. Who gets to decide what any of it is allowed to do to you is still an open argument, and it has happened four times before about four other kinds of media.
